## KQ-A. What the ZPD, scaffolding, and dynamic assessment actually are in the primary sources

### Takeaway
Vygotsky's ZPD is a *diagnostic* concept about maturing psychological functions (development), defined as the gap between independent and assisted problem solving; it is not a recipe for pitching task difficulty, and Chaiklin argues the ed-tech reading ("assistance + slightly-too-hard task = ZPD") rests on assumptions Vygotsky did not make. Scaffolding (Wood, Bruner & Ross 1976) is a separate, later construct whose modern operational core is contingency + fading + transfer of responsibility (van de Pol et al. 2010), and dynamic assessment (Feuerstein; Campione & Brown's graduated prompts) is the one lineage that turned "assisted performance" into a measurable quantity — number of prompts to criterion — with modest but real incremental predictive validity over static tests.

### Cited Findings

**Vygotsky 1978, *Mind in Society* ch. 6 "Interaction between Learning and Development" [theory; primary text, pp. 84–91]** — [scan hosted at FAU](https://home.fau.edu/musgrove/web/vygotsky1978.pdf); [Harvard UP record](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674576292)
- Definition (p. 86): the ZPD "is the distance between the actual developmental level as determined by independent problem solving and the level of potential development as determined through problem solving under adult guidance or in collaboration with more capable peers."
- The two-children example (pp. 85–86): two children "both of whom are ten years old chronologically and eight years old in terms of mental development"; after assistance ("some might run through an entire demonstration and ask the children to repeat it, others might initiate the solution and ask the child to finish it, or offer leading questions") "the first child can deal with problems up to a twelve-year-old's level, the second up to a nine-year-old's." "This difference between twelve and eight, or between nine and eight, is what we call the zone of proximal development."
- What the zone *is* (pp. 86–87): "The zone of proximal development defines those functions that have not yet matured but are in the process of maturation, functions that will mature tomorrow but are currently in an embryonic state. These functions could be termed the 'buds' or 'flowers' of development rather than the 'fruits' of development. The actual developmental level characterizes mental development retrospectively, while the zone of proximal development characterizes mental development prospectively."
- The "tomorrow" claim (p. 87): "what is in the zone of proximal development today will be the actual developmental level tomorrow — that is, what a child can do with assistance today she will be able to do by herself tomorrow." Evidence offered is Dorothea McCarthy's observation that functions 3–5-year-olds "could do only under guidance, in collaboration, and in groups" they "could do independently when they reached the age of five-to-seven years."
- Imitation as the mechanism and its limit (p. 88): "a person can imitate only that which is within her developmental level. For example, if a child is having difficulty with a problem in arithmetic and the teacher solves it on the blackboard, the child may grasp the solution in an instant. But if the teacher were to solve a problem in higher mathematics, the child would not be able to understand the solution no matter how many times she imitated it."
- The "good learning" formula (p. 89): "learning which is oriented toward developmental levels that have already been reached is ineffective from the viewpoint of a child's overall development. It does not aim for a new stage of the developmental process but rather lags behind this process. Thus, the notion of a zone of proximal development enables us to propound a new formula, namely that the only 'good learning' is that which is in advance of development."
- Learning creates the zone (p. 90): "an essential feature of learning is that it creates the zone of proximal development; that is, learning awakens a variety of internal developmental processes that are able to operate only when the child is interacting with people in his environment and in cooperation with his peers. Once these processes are internalized, they become part of the child's independent developmental achievement." And: "learning is not development; however, properly organized learning results in mental development and sets in motion a variety of developmental processes that would be impossible apart from learning."
- Note what is *absent* from ch. 6: no success-rate target, no notion of "slightly harder than current level" as an instructional dose, no fading rule; the zone is defined per child by assisted-vs-unassisted *diagnosis*, and the instructional consequence stated is only that instruction should aim at maturing functions rather than matured ones.

**Chaiklin 2003, "The zone of proximal development in Vygotsky's analysis of learning and instruction" (in Kozulin et al., *Vygotsky's Educational Theory in Cultural Context*, CUP, pp. 39–64) [theory]** — [Cambridge record](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/vygotskys-educational-theory-in-cultural-context/zone-of-proximal-development-in-vygotskys-analysis-of-learning-and-instruction/1F8B412CF2A358988F0E7C2ABBCECBA3)
- Chapter summary (Cambridge page, verbatim): "Vygotsky's concept of zone of proximal development is more precise and elaborated than its common reception or interpretation"; the chapter gives "a comprehensive introduction to and interpretation of this concept, along with comments about predominant contemporary interpretations." Veresov (2004) cites the same sentence at "Chaiklin, 2003, p. 39" — [Veresov, "ZPD: the hidden dimension?"](http://lchc.ucsd.edu/MCA/Mail/xmcamail.2010_04.dir/pdfGIFUwuibFT.pdf).
- Points reachable through a secondary summary (Shabani, Khatib & Ebadi 2010, *English Language Teaching* 3(4), ERIC EJ1081990) — [ERIC full text](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1081990.pdf): "Chaiklin (2003) believes that as a first step for understanding how Vygotsky formulated the zone of proximal development, it is important to remember that Vygotsky's interest is to develop a theoretical basis for appropriate pedagogical interventions … Interventions must be based on diagnostic procedures based on a learner's current state of development." "Chaiklin (2003) points out that a person's ability to imitate, as conceived by Vygotsky, is the basis for a zone of proximal development. Imitation, as used here, is not a mindless copying of actions … imitation presupposes some understanding of the structural relations in a problem that is being solved (Vygotsky 1987, p. 210)." "Chaiklin (2003) highlights that the main focus for collaborative interventions is to find evidence for maturing psychological functions, with the assumption that the learner could only take advantage of these interventions because the maturing function supports an ability to understand the significance of the support being offered."
- The full chapter (the three assumptions of the "common conception" — generality, assistance, potential — and the objective/subjective ZPD distinction) could not be retrieved: ResearchGate 403, Aarhus/Bath portals list metadata only, Semantic Scholar/Unpaywall report the chapter closed. See Gaps.

**Wood, Bruner & Ross 1976, "The role of tutoring in problem solving", *J. Child Psychology and Psychiatry* 17(2):89–100 [lab; preschool, K-12 only]** — [Wiley record](https://acamh.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1469-7610.1976.tb00381.x) (403 to fetchers; ~9,970 citations per [Semantic Scholar](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1111/j.1469-7610.1976.tb00381.x?fields=title,citationCount))
- Six scaffolding functions, as summarized by Margulieux (secondary; the Wiley full text was not reachable): "Recruitment – gain learner interest in correct task completion; Reduction in degrees of freedom – simplify task to match competence; Direction maintenance – keep learner on task and progressing; Marking critical features – provide feedback on incorrect steps; Frustration control – reduce stress without creating emotional dependence; Demonstration – model task portions learner cannot yet complete." — [Margulieux summary](https://laurenmarg.com/2018/12/09/article-summary-wood-et-al-1976-role-of-tutoring-in-problem-solving/)
- Design and age results (same summary): 3-, 4- and 5-year-olds building an interlocking wooden-block pyramid; "3-year-olds: frequently needed goal reminders and technique demonstrations"; "4-year-olds: required more verbal correction … tutoring was 'messiest'"; "5-year-olds: needed confirmation of correct progress; rarely needed help after understanding the task." The summary also records the paper's precondition that learners cannot benefit unless they "can understand the solution, even if they are not yet able to produce the solution independently" — the same imitation-limit idea as Vygotsky p. 88.
- The much-quoted definition ("a process that enables a child or novice to solve a problem, carry out a task or achieve a goal which would be beyond his unassisted efforts", p. 90) is reproduced across the search snippets and secondary literature; it was not read from the paper itself in this pass. Wood et al. do not cite Vygotsky; the ZPD–scaffolding link was made later (Cazden 1979; Bruner 1985) — [secondary, not verified here].

**van de Pol, Volman & Beishuizen 2010, "Scaffolding in teacher–student interaction: a decade of research", *Educational Psychology Review* 22:271–296 [meta/narrative review; K-12 classroom studies]** — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-010-9127-6); [ERIC EJ924182](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ924182); abstract via [Semantic Scholar](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1007/s10648-010-9127-6?fields=abstract)
- Abstract (verbatim): "contingency, fading, and transfer of responsibility are discerned in this review as the three key characteristics of scaffolding … These strategies are synthesized into a framework for analysis, distinguishing between scaffolding means and intentions. Third, the small number of effectiveness studies available is discussed and the results suggest that scaffolding is effective. However, more research is needed. The main challenge in scaffolding research appears to be its measurement."
- No pooled effect size exists in this review; it is narrative. The body-text definitions (contingency = support adapted to the student's current level, established by diagnosis; fading = gradual withdrawal; transfer of responsibility = the student taking over control) were not reachable verbatim (Springer cookie wall; VU repository PDF 403).

**van de Pol, Volman, Oort & Beishuizen 2015, *Instructional Science* 43:615–641 [cRCT; 30 teachers / 768 students aged 12–15, Dutch pre-vocational social studies; K-12 only]** — [ERIC EJ1071539 abstract](https://api.ies.ed.gov/eric/?search=id:EJ1071539&format=json)
- Verbatim: "Teacher scaffolding, in which teachers support students adaptively or 'contingently,' is assumed to be effective. Yet, hardly any evidence from classroom studies exists." "Low contingent support was more effective in promoting students' achievement and task effort than high contingent support in situations where independent working time was low (i.e. help was frequent). In situations where independent working time was high (i.e., help was less frequent), high contingent support was more effective than low contingent support in fostering students' achievement (when correcting for students' task effort). In addition, higher levels of contingent support resulted in a higher appreciation of support. Scaffolding, thus, is not unequivocally effective; its effectiveness depends, among other things, on the independent working time of the groups and students' task effort."
- Contingency was measured (coded from teacher–group interactions), not directly manipulated; the manipulation was a teacher PD programme. Effect coefficients sit in the paywalled body.

**Wood, Wood & Middleton 1978, *Int. J. Behavioral Development* 1(2):131–147 [lab; 32 preschoolers; K-12 only]** — [ERIC EJ189287](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ189287)
- ERIC abstract (complete): "Tests predictions concerning the effectiveness of four different strategies for teaching 3-to 4-year-old children how to master a difficult construction task. Subjects were 32 preschool children." The "contingent shift" rule (more help after failure, less after success) and the result that contingent instruction beat demonstration/verbal/swing strategies on unaided post-test are the standard report of this paper, but no effect size or percentage was reachable from an open source in this pass. Wood & Wood 1999 restate the rule: "more and more help in time of need is one aspect of contingency. The other is in drawing back" — [Wood & Wood 1999 PDF](https://www.tlu.ee/~kpata/haridustehnoloogiaTLU/tutoring.pdf).

**Dynamic assessment (DA)**
- Four families of DA procedures, as catalogued by Vanderbilt's DA guide (secondary): Feuerstein's "open-ended, clinical approach that follows the learner, using generic problem solving tasks such as matrices" (the Learning Potential Assessment Device, LPAD); Budoff/Guthke's "standardized intervention" on generic tasks; Campione & Brown's "graduated prompting procedure where learners are offered increasingly more explicit hints in response to incorrect responses. All learners progress through the same menu of prompts or hints, varying with regard to the number of prompts required for task solution"; Lidz's "curriculum-based approaches that use actual content from the learner's educational program." — [Vanderbilt DA guide](https://researchguides.library.vanderbilt.edu/c.php?g=68756&p=444895)
- Graduated prompts, as used by the Resing group: "A specific structured approach of dynamic testing, in which the number of prompts needed is taken as indication of a child's potential for learning, is the use of graduated prompts techniques (Campione & Brown, 1987; …). In this approach interventions are provided as long as the child is not able to independently solve a problem, according to a hierarchical structured protocol. Some of these protocols include not only specific problem solving prompts but also instructions regarding metacognitive skills such as planning and checking" — [Resing group, J. Education and Training Studies 5(6), 2017, ERIC EJ1141388](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1141388.pdf) (N = 120 second-graders with arithmetic difficulties; K-12).
- Ferrara, Brown & Campione 1986, *Child Development* 57:1087–1099 [lab; 8–11-year-olds; K-12] (abstract via Europe PMC, PMID 3769602): "8-11-year-old children learned to solve letter series completion problems with the aid of graduated sequences of prompts." "Average-IQ (and younger) children required more assistance than high-IQ (and older) children to achieve the same mastery level on the original problem types." "average-versus high-IQ students, and younger versus older children, subsequently diverged in the number of prompts they needed as the transfer problems differed increasingly from the ones originally learned." "assistance required in learning proved quite stable across moderately related tasks (letter series and progressive matrices). Assistance needed in maintenance and transfer appeared less stable but was also significantly correlated across tasks." — [Europe PMC record](https://europepmc.org/abstract/MED/3769602)
- Campione 1989, "Assisted assessment: a taxonomy of approaches…", *J. Learning Disabilities* 22(3) — abstract elided by publisher (Sage 403; Semantic Scholar abstract null). Not read. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/002221948902200303)
- Predictive validity, Caffrey, Fuchs & Fuchs 2008, *J. Special Education* 41(4):254–270 [meta-review; 24 studies, 15 quantified with Pearson r; preschool–high school]: "Results indicated superior predictive validity for DA when feedback is not contingent on student response, when applied to students with disabilities rather than at-risk or typically achieving students, and when independent DA and criterion-referenced tests were used as outcomes instead of norm-referenced tests and teacher judgment." — [ERIC EJ796864](https://api.ies.ed.gov/eric/?search=title:%22predictive%20validity%20of%20dynamic%20assessment%22&format=json). Note the direction: the *standardised* (non-contingent) graduated-prompt format predicts better than clinical contingent mediation.
- Swanson & Lussier 2001, *Review of Educational Research* 71(2):321–363 [meta]: "a meta-analysis of published research on dynamic assessment that reviewed 30 studies (170 effect sizes). Discusses factors that impact effect size, including the predictive ability of type of assessment for effect size." — [ERIC EJ644217](https://api.ies.ed.gov/eric/?search=title:%22selective%20synthesis%20of%20the%20experimental%20literature%20on%20dynamic%20assessment%22&format=json). The overall effect size (commonly cited around d ≈ 1.0 for DA vs static) was NOT verifiable from any open source; treat as unverified.
- L2 dynamic assessment, Poehner & Lantolf 2005, *Language Teaching Research* 9(3) [theory/case; adult L2]: ERIC abstract records the paper "grounded in Vygotsky's Zone of Proximal Development theory", referencing "Reuven Feuerstein's foundational work" and contrasting DA with formative assessment — [ERIC EJ805486](https://api.ies.ed.gov/eric/?search=title:%22Dynamic%20Assessment%20in%20the%20Language%20Classroom%22&format=json). Lantolf & Poehner's interventionist (standardised mediation, quantifiable, psychometric lineage) vs interactionist (open, dialogic, "prioritizes instruction/learning over statistical measurement") distinction is reported consistently across the L2 DA literature — [example, Language Testing in Asia 2022](https://languagetestingasia.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s40468-022-00195-0). No effect sizes exist for interactionist DA; it is a qualitative tradition.

### Inferences
- What an AI tutor can legitimately borrow from Vygotsky is the *diagnostic* move — measure independent performance AND assisted performance and treat the gap as the signal — not a difficulty target. Vygotsky's own text defines the zone by *how much help closes the gap*, which is exactly what Campione & Brown quantified (prompts to criterion). A graduated hint ladder with a count of hints used is therefore the most faithful operationalisation of ZPD available, and it doubles as a learning-potential measure with demonstrated (modest) incremental predictive validity (Caffrey 2008; Ferrara 1986). [inference from primary texts]
- "Only good learning is in advance of development" (p. 89) plus the imitation limit (p. 88) together imply a two-sided constraint: content the learner can already do alone is wasted; content the learner cannot understand even when shown is also wasted. The zone is the band where a demonstration is *comprehensible but not yet reproducible*. That is a comprehension test ("can you follow this worked solution?"), not a success-rate test. [inference]
- Chaiklin's critique (as far as reachable) is that the ZPD is about maturing *functions* across an age period, not about any particular skill or task, so "the ZPD for quadratic equations" is a category error in Vygotsky's terms. Practically this means the ed-tech "ZPD" is really Wood-style scaffolding + Metcalfe-style region of proximal learning, and should be argued from those literatures (which have effect sizes), not from Vygotsky (which has none). [inference; Chaiklin full text not verified]
- van de Pol 2015 is the only classroom experiment on contingency and its headline is a warning, not an endorsement: contingent help raised *appreciation* unconditionally but raised *achievement* only when help was infrequent (long independent working time). For a tutor this means "be contingent" is necessary but not sufficient — the timing/frequency of intervention is a separate lever that can cancel the benefit. [inference from K-12 evidence]

## KQ-B. Trade-offs of aiming for high success while staying in the ZPD

### Takeaway
The two camps are both right on their own outcome measure and the primaries already state the reconciliation: high success (matched materials, assistance for novices, easiest-unlearned items) wins for acquisition, engagement, and low-prior-knowledge learners; errorful attempts win for retention *only* when the attempt is informed (not a blind guess), immediate corrective feedback with the answer follows, and the learner already has some knowledge. The famous numbers — Betts's 95/90, the 85% rule, the "optimal challenge point" — are conventions or models with no human success-rate titration behind them; the one direct human test (motor, N = 192) puts the optimum near 30% error, and the largest engagement dataset (Lomas 2013, ~80K sessions) finds engagement rising monotonically with ease while learning falls.

### Cited Findings

**B1. Instructional-level thresholds (reading) — entirely K-12**
- Betts 1946 as reported by Shanahan: Betts "claimed instructional-level texts should be read with '95–98 percent accuracy (in terms of word reading) and understood with 75–89 percent comprehension.'" "The study Betts referred to as the source of the instructional-level criteria was a doctoral dissertation of one of his students [Killgallon 1942], and that study neither matched books to students for instruction nor evaluated learning." — [Shanahan, American Educator, Summer 2020](https://www.aft.org/ae/summer2020/shanahan) [review/essay]. Shanahan's slide deck: Killgallon's dissertation "examined a small group of 4th graders to find out how many oral reading mistakes they could make and still accomplish 75% reading comprehension" — [Shanahan slide deck PDF](https://www.shanahanonliteracy.com/upload/publications/272/pdf/reading-levels-1.pdf). The "41 fourth-graders" figure was not confirmed in any fetched source. Note the drift: Betts's own instructional band was 95–98% accuracy; the 90–94% instructional / <90% frustration bands are the later IRI convention.
- Shanahan on the evidence base: "it included no evidence showing that teaching at an instructional level gave students any learning advantage." — [Shanahan blog, "Rejecting Instructional Level Theory"](https://www.shanahanonliteracy.com/blog/rejecting-instructional-level-theory-1). His own boundary condition: "No one is claiming that students will just learn more from harder books. As students confront greater amounts of challenge the teaching demands go up."
- Harder text with support: Morgan, Wilcox & Eldredge 2000, *J. Educational Research* [cRCT; 51 second-grade poor readers; 95 sessions of 15-min dyad reading at instructional level vs 2 grades above vs 4 grades above]: "They all improved with dyad reading regardless of the difficulty levels of materials. Results suggest that the difficulty level of materials used for dyad reading may make a difference." — [DOI 10.1080/00220670009598749](https://doi.org/10.1080/00220670009598749); Shanahan's gloss: "Students placed in books above their instructional level had made significantly bigger learning gains." Scaffold was a stronger peer, not a teacher. Kuhn et al. 2006, *J. Literacy Research* [cRCT-quasi; grade 2]: scaffolded repeated reading of grade-level texts and wide reading "showed similar benefits for standardized measures of word reading efficiency and reading comprehension skills compared to control approaches" — [DOI 10.1207/s15548430jlr3804_1](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15548430jlr3804_1). Shanahan's deck also lists O'Connor, Swanson & Geraghty 2010 ("Instructional level placement provided no benefit for students with learning disabilities") and Lupo et al. 2019 (9th grade: "no learning benefit to being placed in the easier texts") — secondary, not fetched.
- The Burns line (supports matched level): Burns 2004, *Remedial and Special Education* [meta; 13 articles, 55 ES; K-12/special ed]: drill ratios "< 50% known, 50% to 69% known, 70% to 85% known, and 90% known … with the 3 least challenging ratios resulting in strong mean effects. The most challenging (< 50% known) ratio resulted in a small to moderate effect. A mean effect size of .82 computed for various student outcomes" — [DOI 10.1177/07419325040250030401](https://doi.org/10.1177/07419325040250030401). Treptow, Burns & McComas 2007 [single-subject, N = 3 third-graders]: "frustration (80-90% known), instructional (93-97% known), and independent levels (100% known) … improved time on task … at the instructional level … Comprehension was highest at the independent level" — [ERIC EJ788336](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ788336). Burns 2007, *School Psychology Quarterly* [cRCT-quasi; 29 third-graders with LD]: pre-teaching unknown words to reach the "93% to 97% known" level; "correlation between number of passages read at the instructional level and reading progress within the curriculum was .80"; responders "65.5% … as compared to 27.6% of the control group" — [ERIC EJ776139](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ776139). Burns 2024, *Assessment for Effective Intervention* [meta; 21 studies, 27 effects; K-12 fluency interventions]: "overall weighted effect size (ES) = 0.43 (95% CI = [0.25, 0.62]) … Using reading passages that represented an instructional level based on accuracy criteria led to a large weighted effect of ES = 1.03, 95% CI = [0.65, 1.40] … reliably larger (p < 0.05) than … rate criteria (weighted ES = 0.29) … largest weighted effect was found for students with reading disabilities (ES = 1.14)." — [ERIC EJ1435507](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1435507). Caveat: fluency outcomes only; single author who is the position's main proponent.
- College/adult evidence on instructional level: none found in any source.

**B2. Region of proximal learning (Metcalfe & Kornell) — college lab**
- Metcalfe 2002, *JEP: General* 131(3):349 [lab; 5 experiments; college + grade 6]: "as more time was given, people shifted toward studying more difficult items. Experts, whether college students or Grade 6 children, devoted their time to items that were more difficult than did novices. However, in a multiple-trials experiment, people regressed toward easier items on Trial 2 … These findings are in opposition to the discrepancy reduction model and support the region of proximal learning hypothesis." — [DOI 10.1037/0096-3445.131.3.349](https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.131.3.349)
- Kornell & Metcalfe 2006, *JEP: LMC* 32(3):609–622 [lab; Columbia undergraduates]: "In Experiment 1, participants' learning was enhanced when they were allowed to control what they studied. Experiments 2a-d replicated this finding … Experiments 3a and 3b showed that, when forced to choose among items they did not know, participants chose the easiest items and benefited from doing so." — [PubMed 16719670](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719670/); [ERIC EJ742317](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ742317). The honored-vs-dishonored recall percentages were not extractable (paywall).
- Metcalfe 2009, *Current Directions* 18(3): "First they attempt to eliminate materials that are already well learned. Then they progress successively from studying easier to more difficult materials. Successful implementation of this metacognitively guided strategy enhances learning. The necessary components are, first, that the metacognitions be accurate, and second, that the appropriate choices are implemented for study." — [DOI 10.1111/j.1467-8721.2009.01628.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8721.2009.01628.x)
- Metcalfe's reconciliation with Bjork (Bjork festschrift chapter, "Desirable Difficulties and Studying in the Region of Proximal Learning"): RPL "proposes that learning is optimized by having the learner study materials that are not very difficult, given the individual's current state of learning. Indeed they should be the easiest possible as-yet-unlearned items — just beyond what the learner has already fully mastered, but not much beyond. Too much difficulty, within this framework, is seen as maladaptive and potentially disheartening." "'desirable' … is key in the desirable difficulties framework — very difficult is not desirable." Cites Nelson & Leonesio's "labor in vain" effect (time on hardest items yields "no memory benefit"). [review chapter; local extracted text, publisher page not fetched]

**B3. Desirable difficulties vs errorless learning — college lab**
- Metcalfe 2017, *Annual Review of Psychology* 68:465–489 [review]: "Although error avoidance during learning appears to be the rule in American classrooms, laboratory studies suggest that it may be a counterproductive strategy, at least for neurologically typical students. Experimental investigations indicate that errorful learning followed by corrective feedback is beneficial to learning … Errors committed with high confidence are corrected more readily than low-confidence errors. Corrective feedback, including analysis of the reasoning leading up to the mistake, is crucial." Boundary conditions from the body: "Huelser & Metcalfe (2012) found a beneficial effect of error generation as long as the errors people produced were not completely unrelated to the target. They found no benefit with unrelated pairs — a finding that was replicated by Grimaldi & Karpicke (2012) and by Knight et al. (2012) … Kang et al. (2011) found no effect when participants had absolutely no idea what the answers to factual questions might be … the guess needs to be somewhat informed rather than a shot in the dark." "People get virtually no benefit unless the feedback they receive provides the correct answer, as Pashler et al. (2005) showed." Amnesia exception: "errors produce enormous interference in amnesics … amnesic patients can benefit from errorless learning." Older adults "corrected high-confidence errors only slightly more than low-confidence errors." Metacognitive blind spot: "Even immediately after they had experienced the task and had evidenced a benefit of 20% … participants thought that the error-free condition had resulted in better recall (Huelser & Metcalfe 2012)." — [Columbia OA PDF](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/psychology/metcalfe/PDFs/Learning%20from%20errorsAnnual%20ReviewMetcalfe2016.pdf)
- Kornell, Hays & Bjork 2009, *JEP: LMC* 35:989–998 [lab; UCLA undergraduates, N = 11–25 per experiment]: "Unsuccessful retrieval attempts enhanced learning with both types of materials … taking challenging tests — instead of avoiding errors — may be one key to effective learning." Final cued recall test-vs-read: Exp 1 .41 vs .31 (t(24) = 2.97); Exp 2 (read-only given >2× study time) .32 vs .32; Exp 3 .71 vs .50; Exp 4 .67 vs .55; Exp 6 .69 vs .60. Items left blank vs answered wrong did not differ — the benefit is from the attempt, not the error per se. — [Williams OA PDF](https://web.williams.edu/Psychology/Faculty/Kornell/Publications/Kornell.Hays.Bjork.2009.pdf)
- Hays, Kornell & Bjork 2012 (not 2013), *JEP: LMC*: "failed tests followed by immediate feedback produced greater retention than did a presentation-only condition. Failed tests followed by delayed feedback, by contrast, did not produce such a benefit" — [DOI 10.1037/a0028468](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0028468) [lab; undergraduates]
- Potts & Shanks 2014, *JEP: General* [lab; adults; novel English words and foreign vocabulary]: "generating errors followed by feedback led to significantly better memory for the correct definition or translation than either reading or making incorrect choices … participants are strikingly unaware of this benefit, judging errorful generation to be a less effective encoding method than reading or incorrect choosing, when in fact it was better." — [DOI 10.1037/a0033194](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0033194). This partially contradicts the "informed guess" boundary (novel words, unrelated errors); Potts & Shanks 2018 attribute the effect to enhanced processing of feedback — [DOI 10.1037/xlm0000637](https://doi.org/10.1037/xlm0000637).
- Replication status: the pretesting/errorful-generation effect is described by Metcalfe as "replicated many times"; boundary conditions (unrelated pairs, no feedback, delayed feedback, amnesia) replicate across labs. Nearly all evidence is college word-pair/trivia with short delays.

**B4. Assistance dilemma — Koedinger & Aleven 2007, *Educational Psychology Review* 19:239–264 [review of Cognitive Tutor experiments; middle/high-school math + college Lisp/chemistry/genetics]**
- "How should learning environments balance information or assistance 'giving' and 'withholding' to achieve optimal student learning? … We call this problem the 'assistance dilemma' and emphasize the need for further science to yield specific conditions and parameters that indicate when and to what extent to use information giving versus information withholding forms of interaction." Link to Bjork: "Paas and Van Merrienboer (1994) found, on one hand, that worked examples made performance easier during instruction and led to better learning and, on the other hand, that introducing variability … made performance harder during instruction but also led to better learning … It does not resolve the assistance dilemma." Evidence: Corbett & Anderson 1995 (college Lisp) "the three feedback conditions led to better and faster learning compared to the no feedback condition … immediate feedback … about three times faster … Withholding of this information was shown to be counterproductive"; help misuse: "students frequently use bottom-out hints to obtain answers, without reading prior hints … even after multiple errors on a step, students often do not request help"; McLaren et al. 2006 added worked examples to a chemistry tutor with "no benefit … replicated with both college and high school students." Conclusion: "within a context of tutored problem solving, information should be withheld very sparingly and that subsequent research on improving tutored problem solving may be more successful if it focuses on methods to give more information rather than methods to withhold it." Caveat: "these strategies were evaluated in a context in which students were engaged in active problem solving, which is an important kind of information/assistance withholding." — [CMU PACT OA PDF](https://pact.cs.cmu.edu/pubs/Koedinger%20Aleven%2007.pdf)
- KLI resolution: Koedinger, Corbett & Perfetti 2012, *Cognitive Science*: "three broad classes of learning events (LEs): (a) memory and fluency processes, (b) induction and refinement processes, and (c) understanding and sense-making processes … different knowledge changes and constraints on optimal instructional choices." — [DOI 10.1111/j.1551-6709.2012.01245.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1551-6709.2012.01245.x) [framework]

**B5. Expertise reversal — mostly higher-education samples**
- Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller 2003, *Educational Psychologist* 38(1):23–31 [review]: "Instructional techniques that are highly effective with inexperienced learners can lose their effectiveness and even have negative consequences when used with more experienced learners. We call this phenomenon the expertise reversal effect." — [DOI 10.1207/s15326985ep3801_4](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep3801_4)
- Tetzlaff et al. 2025 [meta; 60 experiments, 176 ES, 5,924 participants] — full quotes in KQ-D item 7: novices d = 0.505 [0.260, 0.750]; experts d = −0.428 [−0.647, −0.209]; Egger tests n.s. ("z = 0.568 p = .57; z = 0.299 p = .77"); educational status moderated the novice effect (R² = 25.92%), "especially pronounced in higher education and seems to be especially weak in primary schools"; "especially weak in language and humanities"; "providing novices with assistance has a stronger effect than withholding assistance from experts"; "rather provide assistance than to withhold it when in doubt." — [pedocs PDF](https://www.pedocs.de/volltexte/2026/34113/pdf/Learn_and_Instr_2025_Tetzlaff_u.a._A_cornerstone_of_adaptivity.pdf)

**B6. Challenge point framework — adult motor learning**
- Guadagnoli & Lee 2004, *J. Motor Behavior* 36(2):212–224 [theory]: "motor tasks represent different challenges for performers of different abilities. The authors propose that learning is related to the information arising from performance, which should be optimized along functions relating the difficulty of the task to the skill level of the performer." Constructs: nominal vs functional difficulty; "the optimal challenge point will change as the individual's ability to use information changes." — [DOI 10.3200/jmbr.36.2.212-224](https://doi.org/10.3200/jmbr.36.2.212-224)
- Direct test: Akizuki & Ohashi 2015, *Human Movement Science* [lab; adults; four difficulty levels of a postural task]: "Results from the retention test showed an inverted-U relationship between task difficulty during acquisition and motor learning. The second-highest level of task difficulty was the most effective for motor learning, while learning was delayed at the most and least difficult levels." — [DOI 10.1016/j.humov.2015.07.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.humov.2015.07.007)
- Hodges & Lohse 2022, *J. Sports Sciences* 40(7): "surprisingly this framework has not made its way into much of the applied sport science literature" — [DOI 10.1080/02640414.2021.2015917](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2021.2015917). 2025 scoping review of 100 CPF papers notes "lack of practical application research" — [DOI 10.1080/00222895.2025.2508283](https://doi.org/10.1080/00222895.2025.2508283). Never tested with an academic-learning population.

**B7. "Sweet spot" evidence beyond the 85% rule**
- Wilson et al. 2019 — see KQ-D item 10 (model only; optimum shifts "from 85% for Gaussian, to 82% for Laplacian noise, to 75% for Cauchy noise"; Bayesian learners with perfect memory have no sweet spot) — [PMC6831579](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6831579/)
- Al-Fawakhiri, Kayani & McDougle 2023 (bioRxiv; N = 194 adults in the methods, 184 after outlier exclusion for the group comparisons; the abstract says 192; dynamically constrained error rate in a motor task): "learning is greatest at around a ∼30% error rate" — [bioRxiv](https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.07.19.549705) [lab preprint]
- Lomas, Patel, Forlizzi & Koedinger 2013, CHI [large online experiments; children; 10,478 sessions Exp 1, ~70K Exp 2, Battleship Numberline]: "in almost all cases, subjects were more engaged and played longer when the game was easier, which seems to contradict the generality of the Inverted-U Hypothesis. Troublingly, we also found that the most engaging design conditions produced the slowest rates of learning." "our results show a linear relationship between difficulty and engagement (the quadratic fit was not significant, p=0.8)"; easiest conditions' success rate "still <70%". — [DOI 10.1145/2470654.2470668](https://doi.org/10.1145/2470654.2470668). Lomas et al. 2017 "Is Difficulty Overrated?" (>20,000 sessions): "even moderate difficulty can reduce player engagement" — [DOI 10.1145/3025453.3025638](https://doi.org/10.1145/3025453.3025638)
- Difficulty adaptation and interest, *Computers & Education* 2022 [within-subject classroom; 167 Finnish 5th graders]: "difficulty adaptation increased situational interest, but only when the task difficulty was substantially adapted downwards. Contrary to our expectations, a substantial upwards adaptation of the task difficulty significantly decreased situational interest." — [DOI 10.1016/j.compedu.2022.104694](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2022.104694)
- Flow: Fong, Zaleski & Leach 2014, *J. Positive Psychology* [meta; 28 studies]: "the relationship between challenge–skill balance and flow was moderate, and smaller with intrinsic motivation … weaker correlations … in work or education contexts" — [DOI 10.1080/17439760.2014.967799](https://doi.org/10.1080/17439760.2014.967799). Flow studies measure affect, not learning.
- No human study was found that randomly assigned learners to explicit target success rates (e.g. 70 vs 85 vs 95%) in an academic tutoring system.

**B8. Learning vs performance**
- Soderstrom & Bjork 2015, *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 10(2):176–199 (not *Psychological Bulletin*) [review]: "what we can observe and measure is performance, which is often an unreliable index of whether the relatively long-term changes that constitute learning have taken place … improvements in performance can fail to yield significant learning — and, in fact, that certain manipulations can have opposite effects on learning and performance … people often mistakenly interpret their performance during acquisition as a reliable guide to long-term learning." — [DOI 10.1177/1745691615569000](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691615569000)

### Inferences
- The camps split by *phase and outcome*, not by truth: acquisition of a new procedure, novices, engagement, and fluency drill favour high success (Burns 2004/2024, Tetzlaff novices, Koedinger & Aleven, Lomas engagement); retention after acquisition, learners with some knowledge, and transfer favour informed errorful attempts with immediate corrective feedback (Kornell 2009, Hays 2012, Metcalfe 2017, Tetzlaff experts). A tutor should therefore run two regimes per concept — assisted, low-error acquisition, then unassisted, errorful retrieval — rather than one success target. [inference]
- The "informed guess + answer feedback" boundary (Metcalfe 2017; Pashler 2005) is the practical filter for when to let a student fail: if the student has no relevant prior knowledge, failure is inert and should be pre-empted with a worked example; if they have partial knowledge, a failed attempt followed by the correct answer is the higher-yield move. [inference]
- Every optimal-difficulty number in circulation is either a model (85%), a convention (95/90), or a motor-task result (~70%, inverted-U at "second-highest" difficulty). Nothing licenses a specific accuracy target for academic tutoring; a *band* (roughly 65–90% first-attempt success during practice) is consistent with all the human evidence, and engagement data (Lomas) say the lower edge costs engagement, not learning. [extrapolation]
- The recurring metacognitive finding — learners rate errorless and passive conditions as better even when they learned less (Huelser & Metcalfe 2012; Potts & Shanks 2014; Deslauriers 2019) — means learner satisfaction and felt learning cannot be the tutor's control signal for difficulty. [inference]

### Gaps
- Kornell & Metcalfe 2006 honored-vs-dishonored recall percentages; Metcalfe & Kornell 2005 experiment-level numbers — paywalled (APA).
- Kalyuga 2007 review abstract not retrievable; Bjork 1994 chapter reached only through Koedinger & Aleven's paraphrase.
- Killgallon's exact sample size (commonly given as 41) not confirmed.
- No randomized human titration of target success rate in academic content exists as far as could be found; the closest tests are motor (Al-Fawakhiri 2023; Yamada 2025 null; Akizuki 2015 inverted-U).
- Kandemir et al. 2025 (medical students, quadratic difficulty–exam relation) optimum value not extracted.

## KQ-C. Concrete, evidence-based ways to raise success while staying in the ZPD

### Takeaway
Techniques that raise immediate success *without* a documented learning cost are: contingent help paired with enforced independent attempt time, worked-example → backward-faded completion sequences, pre-training on component concepts, subgoal-labelled steps, and learner-timed (on-demand) hints; techniques that raise satisfaction or diagnosis but not learning are frequent contingent help, and confidence/reasoning tiers. Most of the effect sizes are modest (d ≈ 0.3–0.75 on near transfer), most classroom evidence is K-12, and several "classic" numbers (Wood 1978, Atkinson 2003, Mercer 2004) could not be verified from open text.

### Cited Findings

**C1. Contingent scaffolding rule (increase support after failure, decrease after success)**
- Rule and its two halves, Wood & Wood 1999 [correlational; 42 pupils aged 14–15 on a contingent algebra tutor; K-12]: "more and more help in time of need is one aspect of contingency. The other is in drawing back." — [Wood & Wood 1999, Computers & Education 33:153–169](https://www.tlu.ee/~kpata/haridustehnoloogiaTLU/tutoring.pdf)
- Aptitude × process in the same paper: for the low-prior-knowledge group, help-seeking relative to errors correlated with outcomes (partial r = 0.52) but for the high group it was −0.22 ("Fischer z 2.42, P < 0.02"); depth of help: low 0.35 vs high −0.30 (P < 0.04); error frequency: low −0.51 vs high 0.04 (P < 0.06). Conclusion: "those who really do not know much about the domain fare better if they have a tendency to seek help and/or avoid error." But the high group was "more likely to seek help after error (0.27 against 0.11, P < 0.02)" — the learners who benefit most from help ask least. Waiting longer before asking for help correlated with better outcomes (partial r = 0.41). — same source.
- Classroom test: van de Pol et al. 2015 [cRCT; 768 students; K-12] — see KQ-A: contingent support raised achievement only when "independent working time was high (i.e., help was less frequent)"; with frequent help, *low*-contingent support did better on achievement and task effort; contingency raised appreciation regardless. — [ERIC EJ1071539](https://api.ies.ed.gov/eric/?search=id:EJ1071539&format=json)
- Performance-vs-learning: the rule raises effort/appreciation unconditionally; achievement gain is conditional on infrequent intervention. No delayed-retention data in either study.

**C2. Graduated prompt ladders (dynamic assessment)**
- Ferrara, Brown & Campione 1986 [lab; 8–11-year-olds]: number of prompts to mastery separated average- from high-IQ and younger from older children, and "assistance required in learning proved quite stable across moderately related tasks" — [Europe PMC 3769602](https://europepmc.org/abstract/MED/3769602). Caffrey, Fuchs & Fuchs 2008 [meta-review; 24 studies; K-12]: DA adds predictive validity, most when "feedback is not contingent on student response" — [ERIC EJ796864](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ796864).
- Reading of the trade: graduated prompts are documented as *measurement* (learning potential), not as an intervention with a learning effect size; the modern dynamic-testing studies (e.g., Resing group 2017, N = 120 second-graders) report profiles of instructional need, not gains vs control — [ERIC EJ1141388](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1141388.pdf).

**C3. Worked example → fading → completion problems**
- Renkl, Atkinson, Maier & Staley 2002, *J. Experimental Education* 70(4):293–315 [Exp 1 cRCT-quasi, two 9th-grade Hauptschule classes (n = 20 fading / 15 pairs), physics; Exp 2 lab, "54 American psychology students" (27/27), probability; Exp 3 lab, "45 American students enrolled in several educational psychology courses" (15 × 3) — college-adult in Exp 2–3]. Abstract: "(a) the fading procedure fosters learning, at least when near transfer performance is considered; (b) the number of problem-solving errors during learning plays a role in mediating this effect; and (c) it is more favorable to fade out worked-out solution steps in a backward manner (omitting the last solution steps first)". Exp 1 near transfer "F(1, 32) = 4.44, p < .05 (adjusted means = 9.52 for fading and 7.47 for example-problem pairs) … partial η² = .12"; far transfer n.s. (partial η² = .07). Exp 2 near transfer "F(1, 51) = 4.58, p < .05 (adjusted means = 10.00 for fading and 7.59 …) partial η² = .08"; far transfer "negligible effect, partial η² = .01"; with correctness during learning covaried the group effect vanished — the near-transfer gain is mediated by fewer errors during practice. Learning time equal (31.15 vs 30.37 min). Exp 3: fading vs pairs near "F(1, 42) = 9.88 … adjusted means = 11.41 … 7.13, partial η² = .19"; far "F(1, 42) = 5.45, p < .05 … partial η² = .12"; backward-fading vs control on far transfer "F(1, 27) = 10.20 … adjusted means = 7.70 … 4.24 … partial η² = .27"; forward fading n.s. on far transfer (η² = .04) and cost "over 6" minutes more. — [author-hosted PDF](http://www.davidlewisphd.com/courses/EDD8121/readings/2002-Renkl_et_al.pdf)
- Atkinson, Renkl & Merrill 2003, *JEP* 95(4):774–783 [lab; college]: fading + principle-identification prompts "produced medium to large effects on near and far transfer without requiring additional time on task" — [ERIC EJ678596](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ678596); exact ds paywalled.
- Completion problems: Paas 1992, *JEP* 84(4):429–434 [lab; "46 Dutch secondary school students"; K-12]: results "support the superiority of the alternatives, particularly the completion strategy, in solving statistics problems" — [ERIC EJ456636](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ456636). van Merriënboer 1990, *J. Educational Computing Research* 6(3) [cRCT; Dutch secondary programming]: completion (modify/extend existing programs) vs generation strategy — [ERIC EJ417022](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ417022); numbers not in the record.
- Performance-vs-learning: fading raises near transfer by *reducing errors during practice*; far-transfer benefit appears only with backward fading and only in one of three experiments. No retention cost reported, but delays were short. Small cells (15–27).

**C4. Pre-training / prerequisite repair**
- Mayer & Pilegard 2014 (Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning, 2nd ed., ch. 13) [meta/review; mostly college, short lab lessons, immediate transfer tests]: pre-training principle "people learn more deeply from a multimedia message when they know the names and characteristics of the main concepts", supported "in 13 out of 16 tests, median effect size of 0.75" (segmenting 10/10, median 0.79). — [Cambridge chapter record](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-managing-essential-processing-in-multimedia-learning-segmenting-pretraining-and-modality-principles/DD24C2F48B9B1277CE59F78276110258). The 2005 edition reported 7/7 tests, median 0.92 — the effect has shrunk as tests accumulated.
- Mayer, Mathias & Wetzell 2002, *JEP: Applied* 8(3):147–154 [lab; college]: "Students in the pre-training group performed better than did students in other groups on tests of transfer (in all 3 experiments) and retention (in Experiments 1 and 2)"; a post-training control did not match pre-training — order matters. — [PubMed 12240927](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12240927/)
- Performance-vs-learning: no cost documented; outcomes are transfer, i.e. learning.

**C5. Splitting a step into sub-goals (subgoal labelling)**
- Margulieux, Morrison & Decker 2020, *Int. J. STEM Education* 7:19 [cRCT by section; 265 CS1 students; college]: "Learning subgoals consistently improved performance on quizzes, which were formative and given within a week of learning a new procedure, but not on exams, which were summative. While exam performance was not statistically better, the subgoal group had lower variance in exam scores and fewer students dropped or failed the course than in the control group." Quizzes "subgoal group (M = 48%, SD = 14%) outscored the control group (M = 41%, SD = 16%) with a medium effect size, d = 0.44"; average exam "(M = 75%, SD = 15%) … (M = 72%, SD = 18%) … d = 0.20, U = 7975, p = .24"; total exam "d = 0.26, t(264) = 4.20, p = .04"; failing "almost double in the control group (n = 64, 44%) as in the subgoal group (n = 30, 25%)"; "students in the subgoal group were half as likely to withdraw and half as likely to fail than the control group." — [NSF PAR accepted manuscript](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10171482); [ERIC EJ1253101](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1253101)
- Margulieux, Guzdial & Catrambone 2012 (ICER; adults learning App Inventor) [lab]: subgoal-labelled materials "improved the performance of participants on novel tasks" — [DOI 10.1145/2361276.2361291](https://doi.org/10.1145/2361276.2361291); means not reachable. Catrambone 1998 *JEP: General* 127(4) — not open.
- Performance-vs-learning: the clearest documented case of "immediate success up (d = 0.44), delayed mean unchanged (d = 0.20 n.s.), but the lower tail protected (withdraw/fail halved)".

**C6. Just-in-time / on-demand hints**
- Razzaq & Heffernan 2010, ITS 2010 [cRCT crossover; 72 8th-graders, one class period; K-12]: "students learned significantly more [F(59, 1) = 4.42, p = 0.04] from hints-on-demand and having control over when to ask for a hint (mean gain score = 0.137) compared to having the computer control when to give a hint (mean gain score = 0.04). The effect size of 0.35"; benefit concentrated in high-hint-users ("F(29, 1) = 7.358, p = 0.01"), n.s. for low-hint-users. — [WPI PDF](https://web.cs.wpi.edu/~nth/pubs_and_grants/papers/2010/ITS/Razzaq%20Hints%20Is%20It%20Better%20to%20Give%20or%20Wait.pdf)
- Aleven, Stahl, Schworm, Fischer & Wallace 2003, *RER* 73(3) [review]: "although effective help-seeking behavior in ILEs is related to better learning outcomes, learners are not using help facilities effectively." — [ERIC EJ782605](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ782605)
- Roll, Aleven, McLaren & Koedinger 2011, *Learning and Instruction* 21:267–280 [cRCT; Geometry Cognitive Tutor, high school]: the Help Tutor "improved students' help-seeking" and the improvement "transferred to new domain content in the following month", but domain learning did not differ from control — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2010.07.004) (domain-null caveat from the paper body, not quoted verbatim here).
- Performance-vs-learning: learner-timed hints beat pushed hints on gain; Wood & Wood 1999 adds that low-knowledge learners under-seek, so an error-triggered *offer* (not a pushed hint) is the defensible compromise.

**C7. Confidence and reasoning tiers**
- Certainty-based marking (Gardner-Medwin; UCL medical students): scheme C = 1/2/3 → +1/+2/+3 if correct, 0/−2/−6 if wrong; thresholds "C=1 … less than 67%, C=2 if it is 67-80%, and C=3 if it is greater than 80%"; no controlled learning outcome in the chapter — [Gardner-Medwin 2006 chapter PDF](https://www.tmedwin.net/~ucgbarg/tea/innovass7.pdf)
- Controlled test: "Certainty-based marking in a formative assessment improves student course appreciation but not summative examination scores", *BMC Medical Education* 2019 [cRCT crossover; 389 medical/biomedical students; college-adult]: "Student examination scores, however, were not affected by CBL." — [PMC6544949](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6544949/)
- Two-tier diagnostic tests (Treagust et al. 2011, N = 190, ERIC EJ923504) are used as *outcome instruments* for conceptual-change teaching; no study found in which adding a reasoning tier changed learning vs a one-tier control — [ERIC EJ923504](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ923504)
- Verdict: tiers improve calibration and diagnosis; the one controlled test of learning is null.

**C8. Peer / dialogic support**
- Mercer, Dawes, Wegerif & Sams 2004, *BERJ* 30(3) [cRCT-quasi; UK Year 5; K-12]: "(a) children can be enabled to use talk more effectively as a tool for reasoning and (b) talk based activities can have a useful function in scaffolding the development of reasoning and scientific understanding" — [ERIC EJ680363](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ680363). Wegerif, Mercer & Dawes 1999, *Learning and Instruction* 9:493–516 [cRCT-quasi; "64 8- and 9-year olds"]: "Teaching exploratory talk also improved results on a nonverbal reasoning test." — [ERIC EJ596349](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ596349). Raven's/SAT gain numbers sit in paywalled bodies.
- Howe & Abedin 2013, *Cambridge J. Education* 43(3) [review of 225 studies 1972–2011]: "much more is known about how classroom dialogue is organized than about whether certain modes of organization are more beneficial than others." — [ERIC EJ1022618](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1022618)
- College evidence: Smith et al. 2009, *Science* 323:122–124 [in-class experiment; ~350 intro-genetics students]: "of the students who answered the first question incorrectly before group discussion but correctly after discussion, 77% answered the isomorphic question correctly"; "44% students who answered the first question incorrectly both before and after group discussion were able to answer the second question correctly" — [PMC commentary quoting the paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3140391/). Crouch & Mazur 2001, *Am. J. Phys.* 69(9) [longitudinal cohorts; Harvard]: FCI normalized gain 0.25 (traditional, 1990) → 0.49 (first PI year) → 0.74 (1997); discussion helps "as long as the initial percentage of correct answers to a ConcepTest is between 35% and 70%" and "improvement is largest when the initial percentage of correct answers is around 50%"; "students changed from correct to incorrect answers during discussion only 6% of the time" — [Crouch & Mazur PDF](http://web.mit.edu/jbelcher/www/TEALref/Crouch_Mazur.pdf)
- Performance-vs-learning: peer discussion's gain survives an isomorphic re-test in the same session (Smith 2009); no delayed test. The 35–70% initial-correct window is itself an empirical "ZPD" for peer scaffolding.

### Inferences
- The only technique above with a *delayed*, course-level outcome in adults is subgoal labelling (Margulieux 2020), and it shows the performance/learning split cleanly: formative success up, summative mean flat, dropout/fail halved. An AI tutor should expect and report the same shape — raising in-session success is a retention-of-students lever more than a retention-of-knowledge lever. [inference]
- Renkl's mediation result (near-transfer benefit disappears when errors-during-practice is covaried) says fading works *because* it keeps error rate low during acquisition — direct support for an "errorless-ish acquisition, then errorful retrieval" sequencing rather than a single success target. [inference]
- The two contingency papers (Wood & Wood 1999; van de Pol 2015) converge on frequency/timing as the hidden variable: help that is contingent but too frequent buys appreciation, not achievement; low-knowledge learners under-request. Design rule: contingent ladder + minimum independent-attempt time + error-triggered *offer* for low-prior-knowledge learners only. [inference from K-12 data]

- Not verified verbatim (paywall/fetch block): Wood 1978 post-test percentages; van de Pol 2015 interaction coefficients; Swanson & Lussier 2001 overall d; Atkinson 2003 ds; Paas 1992 means; Catrambone 1998; Margulieux 2012 means; Mercer 2004 Raven's/SAT gains; Roll 2011 domain-learning null (body text); Resnick/Michaels accountable-talk outcomes.
- No study found that tests a *confidence tier* or *reasoning tier* as an intervention on delayed learning other than the null BMC Med Educ 2019 trial.
- K-12-only: Wood 1978, van de Pol 2014/2015, Roll 2011, Razzaq 2010, Wood & Wood 1999, Mercer/Wegerif, Ferrara 1986, Caffrey 2008, Paas 1992, van Merriënboer 1990. College-adult: Renkl 2002 Exp 2–3, Atkinson 2003, Mayer 2002/2014, Margulieux 2012/2020, Crouch & Mazur 2001, Smith 2009, CBM medical trials.

## KQ-D. Re-verification of the earlier pass's numbers

### Takeaway
Of 14 claims, 10 are confirmed verbatim from primary text (Wisniewski, Bisra, Kobayashi 2019, Sinha & Kapur core figures, Tetzlaff, Deslauriers, Wilson, Kang, Hestenes & Halloun, Chi — several with scope caveats: Kang's d = 0.41 is one feedback-dependent cell, Chi's "equal" is an n = 11 null), 2 are corrected (Rey 2019 journal + row labels; Choi 2011 pool-specific item counts), 2 are partially confirmed with the remaining numbers secondary-only (VanLehn's 0.40/0.31; Kulik's 0.52/0.08), and three sub-claims attached to them are corrected (Slavin 1987 medians 0.225/0.31/0.27; "Kobayashi 2022 ERR" is Ribosa & Duran 2022, g = 0.17; Sinha & Kapur fidelity is a 0.37–0.58 range). The 85% rule has now been tested on humans (2023–2026) and did not replicate at 15.87%: the closest direct test (Al-Fawakhiri, which has a 15%-error arm) finds ~30% error optimal; of the other three, one is null (Yamada, golf putting), one finds more learning at moderate than high success (van der Kooij, 50% vs 80% target) and one reports a significant quadratic difficulty effect (Kandemir). The 50%-vs-80% and golf success-rate comparisons have no ~85%-success arm, so they do not directly test the 15.87% target.

### Cited Findings

1. **VanLehn 2011 — PARTIALLY CONFIRMED.** Abstract (verbatim): "when compared to No tutoring, the effect sizes of answer-based tutoring systems, intelligent tutoring systems, and adult human tutors are believed to be d = 0.3, 1.0, and 2.0 respectively. This review did not confirm these beliefs. Instead, it found that the effect size of human tutoring was much lower: d = 0.79. Moreover, the effect size of intelligent tutoring systems was 0.76, so they are nearly as effective as human tutoring." — [ERIC EJ946764 via API](https://api.ies.ed.gov/eric/?search=id:EJ946764&format=json); [OpenAlex abstract](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1080/00461520.2011.611369). Human 0.79 and ITS/step-based 0.76 confirmed. Substep 0.40 and answer-based 0.31: COULD NOT VERIFY from primary (ASU-hosted PDF behind Cloudflare Access; T&F 403; no OA copy in OpenAlex) — the "0.3" in the abstract is the *prior belief*, not the finding. Tier: narrative review with unweighted means, mostly college STEM. [secondary for 0.40/0.31]
2. **Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns 1990 — PARTIALLY CONFIRMED / numbers secondary.** Abstract (verbatim, [OpenAlex](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.3102/00346543060002265)): "A meta-analysis of findings from 108 controlled evaluations showed that mastery learning programs have positive effects on the examination performance of students in colleges, high schools, and upper grades elementary schools. The effects appear to be stronger on weaker students in a class … self-paced mastery programs often reduce the completion rates in college classes." The 0.52 overall / 0.08 standardized-test pair is not in the abstract (Sage 403). Nearest primary-adjacent: Slavin 1990, "Mastery Learning Re-Reconsidered", RER 60(2): "Kulik et al. (1990) agree with what was to me the key finding of my review, that the effects of mastery learning on standardized measures are effectively zero." and "Kulik et al. estimate a mean effect size of +.51 for all LFM (group-based mastery learning) studies in elementary and secondary schools. If they had used medians, their studies would yield an estimate of +.43." — [Slavin 1990 PDF](http://competencyworks.pbworks.com/w/file/fetch/70372686/1170613.pdf). **Slavin 1987 CORRECTED:** his medians were "+.225 for equal-time studies and +.31 for extra-time studies, or +.27 overall" on experimenter-made tests, not "~0.25" (same PDF); K-12 only, best-evidence synthesis.
3. **Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie 2020 — CONFIRMED.** "A meta-analysis (435 studies, k = 994, N > 61,000)"; "a medium effect (d = 0.48)"; "17% of the effects were negative"; high-information feedback "d = 0.99"; "Simple forms of reinforcement and punishment have low effects" "d = 0.24". Caveats from the paper: unpruned random-effects mean was 0.55, reduced to 0.48 after excluding 3.5% extreme effects; I² = 86.47%; the 0.24 category is "reinforcement or punishment", not praise alone; median publication year 1985. — [PMC6987456](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6987456/). [meta; mixed K-12 and college]
4. **Bisra et al. 2018 — CONFIRMED.** "identified 69 effect sizes (from 64 research reports) … The overall weighted mean effect size using a random effects model was g = .55"; body: "69 effect sizes (5917 participants) … g = .55 with a 95% confidence interval ranging from .45 to .65", I² = 65.42. Education-level moderator n.s.: undergraduate k = 42 g = .611; high school g = .426; elementary g = .483. — [ERIC EJ1186664](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1186664); [full text mirror](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2018-bisra.pdf). [meta; majority college]
5. **Kobayashi 2019 — CONFIRMED; "Kobayashi 2022 ERR" CORRECTED.** "The synthesis of 28 studies indicated that the estimated effect sizes (Hedges' gs) were 0.35 for preparing-to-teach and 0.56 for teaching with preparing-to-teach"; "effective in promoting deep learning (as well as surface learning) even after a delay." — [Wiley OA PDF](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdfdirect/10.1111/jpr.12221). The 2022 *Educational Research Review* meta-analysis on generating teaching materials is **Ribosa & Duran 2022** (37:100475), not Kobayashi: "23 articles … 62 comparisons … with an overall effect of 0.17" (g = 0.17, CI 0.04–0.31 as cited in [Kobayashi 2023, PMC9979973](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9979973/)). — [DOI 10.1016/j.edurev.2022.100475](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2022.100475). [meta; mostly college lab studies]
6. **Sinha & Kapur 2021 — CONFIRMED core; fidelity CORRECTED; participants unverified.** "a meta-analysis of 53 studies with 166 comparisons … a significant, moderate effect in favor of PS-I (Hedge's g 0.36 [95% confidence interval 0.20; 0.51]). The effects were even stronger (Hedge's g ranging between 0.37 and 0.58) when PS-I was implemented with high fidelity to the principles of Productive Failure (PF)"; "Contrasting trends were, however, observed for younger age learners (second to fifth graders) and for the learning of domain-general skills, for which effect sizes favored I-PS."; "an estimation of true effect sizes after accounting for publication bias suggested a strong effect size favoring PS-I (Hedge's g 0.87)." — [Semantic Scholar record of DOI 10.3102/00346543211019105](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.3102/00346543211019105?fields=abstract). Fidelity is a *range* 0.37–0.58, not "≈0.58". ">12,000 participants" not in the abstract; unverified. Caveat: authors are PF's originators; a publication-bias adjustment that raises the estimate (0.87) is unusual. [meta; mixed K-12/college]
7. **Tetzlaff, Simonsmeier, Peters & Brod 2025 — CONFIRMED (read from the pedocs CC-BY copy).** "Of 1590 identified studies, 176 effect sizes from 60 experimental studies and a total of 5924 participants were finally included"; "Low prior knowledge learners learn better from high-assistance instruction (d = 0.505). High prior knowledge learners learn better from low-assistance instruction (d = −0.428)"; body: "(d = 0.505, 95 % CI [0.260, 0.750], k = 88, j = 60) … I² = 90.87 %" and "(d = −0.428, 95 % CI [−0.647, −0.209], k = 88, j = 60) … I² = 87.55 %"; "the instructional implication is to rather provide assistance than to withhold it when in doubt"; "the Expertise Reversal Effect is less pronounced in primary school and more prevalent in higher education samples"; "regular assessment is necessary to optimally adjust instruction to a specific learner at a specific point in time". Sample sizes "ranged from 17 to 357 with a median of 73"; median publication year 2013. — [pedocs full text](https://www.pedocs.de/volltexte/2026/34113/pdf/Learn_and_Instr_2025_Tetzlaff_u.a._A_cornerstone_of_adaptivity.pdf); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2025.102142). [meta of experiments; mixed, strongest in higher ed]
8. **Rey et al. 2019 segmenting — CORRECTED (journal and labels).** Journal is *Educational Psychology Review* (not Educational Research Review), DOI 10.1007/s10648-018-9456-4. Table 3 (verbatim): "Overall effect Retention 67 6100 0.32*** [0.20, 0.43] Transfer 56 4754 0.36*** [0.24, 0.48]"; "System-paced segmentation Retention 32 2578 0.42*** [0.21, 0.63] Transfer 30 2890 0.35*** [0.16, 0.54]"; "Learner-paced segmentation Retention 21 2351 0.19 [−0.04, 0.45] Transfer 16 1190 0.45*** [0.24, 0.66]". So 0.45 is learner-paced *transfer* and 0.35 is system-paced *transfer*; on *retention* the pattern reverses (system-paced 0.42 sig., learner-paced 0.19 n.s.). Prior-knowledge moderator on retention: "no prior domain knowledge was d = 0.29 … with at least some prior knowledge was d = −0.12 … with high prior knowledge was d = 0.73". — [author-hosted PDF](https://maria-wirzberger.de/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/Rey2019_Article_AMeta-analysisOfTheSegmentingE.pdf). [meta; mostly college lab multimedia]
9. **Deslauriers et al. 2019 — CONFIRMED.** "students in active classrooms scored almost half an SD (0.46) higher" (P < 0.001); "students in active classrooms reported more than half an SD (0.56) lower FOL compared with those in passive lectures"; "a total of 149 students participated"; randomized crossover across two consecutive class meetings in week 12, Harvard calculus-based mechanics. — [PMC6765278](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6765278/). Replication flag: Boedeker et al. 2025, *Medical Science Educator* (146 medical students, two-day crossover): "scored 0.27 standard deviations higher on the test of learning (p = 0.010) … The feeling of learning was 0.56 standard deviations higher in the large-group interactive session" — the learning gain replicated smaller and the feeling-of-learning went the *opposite* direction. — [DOI 10.1007/s40670-024-02219-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40670-024-02219-1). [cRCT; college]
10. **Wilson et al. 2019 "85% rule" — CONFIRMED as model-only; human follow-ups found.** "the optimal error rate for training is around 15.87% or, conversely, that the optimal training accuracy is about 85%"; noise "sampled from a zero-mean Gaussian distribution"; demonstrated on a perceptron, a two-layer network on MNIST, and the Law & Gold model; "in these more complex situations, our assumptions may not always be met". No human data. — [PMC6831579](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6831579/). Later human tests: (a) Al-Fawakhiri, Kayani & McDougle 2023 (bioRxiv, N = 194 in the methods, 184 after outlier exclusion, 192 in the abstract; motor task with dynamically constrained error rate): "we observed evidence that learning is greatest at around a ∼30% error rate, matching our theoretical predictions" — [bioRxiv 10.1101/2023.07.19.549705](https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.07.19.549705) [lab preprint; adults]; (b) Yamada et al. 2025, *RQES*, 150 adults, golf putting at 60–70% / 20–30% / <10% success: "the pattern and magnitude of learning did not differ, suggesting that no optimal difficulty existed" — [DOI 10.1080/02701367.2025.2587799](https://doi.org/10.1080/02701367.2025.2587799) [lab; null; no 85% arm]; (c) van der Kooij et al. 2026, *Scientific Reports*: "more motor learning in the moderate success group compared to the high success group" [abstract snippet]; (d) Kandemir et al. 2025, *J. Computer Assisted Learning* (medical students): "significant negative quadratic effect of mean relative difficulty on final exam performance" [abstract snippet; optimum value not verified]. Net: no human study confirms 15.87%; the one direct test puts the optimum near 30% error; all human tests are motor or coarse-grained, none is category/language learning with an 85% arm.
11. **Kang, McDermott & Roediger 2007 — CONFIRMED, with scope.** Exp 2 (with feedback), final multiple-choice test: "the intervening SA test condition yielded higher final performance than the intervening MC test, read statements, and control conditions, t(47) = 2.55, d = 0.41; t(47) = 3.23, d = 0.62; t(47) = 6.24, d = 1.18, respectively." On the final short-answer test SA-vs-MC was n.s.; in Exp 1 (no feedback) MC beat SA ("t(47) = 2.14, d = 0.36"); feedback × format interaction "F(1, 94) = 11.44". n = 48 undergraduates per experiment, 3-day delay. — [Roediger-lab PDF](http://psychnet.wustl.edu/memory/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Kang-et-al-2007_EJCP.pdf). So d = 0.41 is one cell (SA vs MC initial test, MC final, with feedback), not a general format effect. [lab; college]
12. **Choi, Grady & Dodd 2011 — CORRECTED in detail.** "A New Stopping Rule for Computerized Adaptive Testing", EPM 71(1). "Two real pools of 28 and 95 items, respectively, served as models to generate small (30) and large (90) item pools"; "The minimum standard error threshold was set equal to 0.3."; CAT "restricted in length between a minimum of three and a maximum of 15 items"; mean items administered: 30-item mismatched PSER 5.519 vs SE 6.154; 90-item mismatched 5.756 vs 5.681; 30-item matched 4.182 vs 3.993; 90-item matched 4.190 vs 3.754. "For a theta level of −2, the minimum SE stopping rule would administer all 28 items in the pool, even though the desired standard error is unobtainable." — [PMC3028267](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3028267/). The "4–6 items" range is right; the pool-specific pairing in the earlier note is not; the item bank is a PROMIS *depression* scale, not an achievement test. [sim; adult PRO]
13. **Hestenes & Halloun 1995 — CONFIRMED.** "we judge the probability of a false negative to be certainly less than ten percent (fewer than three questions 'missed')"; "even random choices have a 20% chance of false positives"; "we interpret an FCI score of 85% as the Newtonian Mastery threshold"; "We suggest an FCI score of 60% as the entry threshold to Newtonian physics." — [InterFCI.pdf](https://davidhestenes.net/modeling/R&E/InterFCI.pdf). Tier: expert judgment in a rebuttal to Huffman & Heller's factor analysis, not a measured error rate. [theory/expert; HS + college physics]
14. **Chi et al. 2001 — CONFIRMED, with power caveat.** Abstract: "tutors were suppressed from giving explanations and feedback. Instead, tutors were encouraged to prompt the students. Surprisingly, students learned just as effectively even when tutors were suppressed from giving explanations and feedback." Body: "Table 4 shows the mean proportion correct for each category of questions for the tutored and the prompted students. There were no significant differences among the means (controlling for pretest and reading scores). Thus, eliminating tutor explanations and reducing tutor feedback did not hinder the prompted students' learning of either the shallow or the deep knowledge." Tutors' statements fell from 621 to 178 on average ("t(20) = 5.59"). n = 11 tutors, 11 eighth-grade tutees per study; Study 1 vs Study 2 is a between-study comparison a week apart with no control group; "tutored students were able to learn or recall 75% (127/169) of what the tutors explained to them, whereas the prompted students were able to construct on their own, only 44% (75/169) of the answers". — [ASU-hosted PDF](https://education.asu.edu/sites/g/files/litvpz656/files/lcl/image3_2.pdf). [lab; K-12 tutees, college tutors; n = 11/condition — "equal" is an underpowered null]

### Inferences
- The earlier pass's headline numbers survive except where it compressed a table into a slogan: Rey's "learner-paced beats system-paced" is true for transfer and false for retention; Kang's d = 0.41 holds only with feedback and only on an MC final; Chi's "equal learning" is an n = 11 null. Design decisions leaning on those three should be re-weighted. [inference]
- The 85% rule should now be labelled "model result; human tests 2023–2026 negative or divergent (~30% error optimum in the one direct test)". Using ~85% as a *comfort-band* heuristic remains defensible on the Metcalfe/Bjork literature, not on Wilson. [inference]

### Gaps
- VanLehn's 0.40 (substep) and 0.31 (answer-based) and Kulik's 0.52/0.08 remain secondary-only; the ASU PDF and Sage full text were not reachable.
- Sinha & Kapur's ">12,000 participants" and Tetzlaff's per-education-level effect sizes (only the direction is quoted) were not extracted.
- Adesope 2017 test-format moderators (a possible independent source of a "SA > MC" d) were not reachable.
