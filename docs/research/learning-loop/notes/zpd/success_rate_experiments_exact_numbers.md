# Exact per-condition results of human experiments manipulating target success rate in adaptive practice

Scope note: every number below was read from the primary full text unless marked otherwise. Where only an abstract or a secondary version (e.g., a dissertation chapter) was retrievable, that is stated. Population (children vs adults) is stated per study.

## Q1. Jansen et al. 2013 (Math Garden, 60/75/90% success-rate RCT) + Klinkenberg et al. 2011 (how the 75% target is implemented)

### Takeaway
Children (grades 3-6, n=207 analysed) randomized to Math Garden with pre-set success rates of 60/75/90% achieved 66/72/81% correct; the easiest condition attempted the most problems (645.7 vs 548.1 vs 423.8) and was the only condition to improve addition-subtraction significantly more than the (no-practice) control, and the success-rate -> performance effect was fully mediated by number of problems attempted (indirect effect -.45, bootstrap 95% CI -1.50 to -.03, reported with a negative sign but interpreted by the authors as "increasing success rate indirectly increased math improvement"). Math anxiety fell equally in all four conditions (incl. control); perceived competence did not differ by condition.

### Cited Findings
Source for all Jansen items: full text mirrored at [pdfcoffee](https://pdfcoffee.com/the-influence-of-experiencing-success-in-math-on-math-anxiety-perceived-math-competence-and-math-performance-pdf-free.html) (Learning and Individual Differences 24:190-197, [DOI 10.1016/j.lindif.2012.12.014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lindif.2012.12.014); ERIC record [EJ1008332](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1008332)).

- Population: children aged 8-13, grades 3-6, two Dutch primary schools; "A total of 252 children participated"; "39 children were absent from one of the measurements. Data from six children in experimental conditions were excluded because they had attempted fewer than seven problems ... The final sample consisted of 207 children" (Section 2.1, p.190-191). Attrition therefore = 45/252 (17.9%); the paper does not report attrition per condition.
- Table 1 (p.191), n / mean age (SD) / % male / n in grades 3,4,5,6: Difficult 51 / 10.8 (1.3) / 61% / 15,11,12,13; Medium 52 / 10.7 (1.3) / 54% / 15,11,15,11; Easy 48 / 10.9 (1.3) / 48% / 10,11,16,11; Control 56 / 10.8 (1.3) / 50% / 12,14,16,14; Total 207 / 10.8 (1.3) / 53% / 52,47,59,49. Conditions did not differ in age (F(3,206)=0.20, p=.893) or gender (chi2(3)=1.96, p=.582).
- Design (Section 2.2): "Children from various conditions were in one classroom"; practice period six weeks, teachers told 3-5 sessions/week of 10-15 min; "Weekly practice was recommended but was not registered"; pre-post interval 11.1 weeks (SD 0.36); control = regular curriculum only, received log-ins after the posttest (wait-list).
- Manipulation (Section 2.3.4): "Problems that a participant could solve correctly with a pre-set average percentage correct of 60%, 75%, and 90% were selected for conditions Difficult, Medium, and Easy, respectively. Since the ability estimate of the participant was updated after every problem, these percentages could be maintained throughout the study". Games: 15 problems each, 20 s time limit, coins for speed, coins lost for errors (Maris & Van der Maas 2012 scoring rule).
- Manipulation check (Section 3.1, Table 2): achieved success rates 66% (SD 8%), 72% (6%), 81% (7%) for Difficult/Medium/Easy. Difficult was significantly ABOVE its 60% target (t(50)=5.05, p<.001, d=.70); Medium and Easy significantly BELOW target (Medium t(51)=-3.37, p=.001, d=.47; Easy t(47)=-9.44, p<.001, d=1.37). Conditions still differed (F(2,150)=60.06, p<.001, r=.67; linear trend F(1,150)=119.31).
- Number of problems attempted (Table 2): Difficult 423.8 (SD 334.2), Medium 548.1 (375.2), Easy 645.7 (390.2). Regression on condition (1/2/3): F(1,150)=9.13, p=.003, r=.24, B=111.08 ("the higher the success rate, the more problems were attempted"). No gender difference in problems attempted (t(150)=-1.11, p=.27).
- Math anxiety (MASC-NL, range 23-92; Table 2 pre -> post): Difficult 39.0 (10.5) -> 37.1 (10.6); Medium 42.5 (14.0) -> 37.1 (10.1); Easy 38.2 (12.0) -> 34.8 (10.3); Control 40.0 (13.4) -> 38.0 (13.2). Anxiety dropped significantly in all conditions (CI excluded zero for Medium, Easy, Control; upper bound at zero for Difficult); no condition effect, Kruskal-Wallis H(3)=3.70, p=.296.
- Perceived math competence (Harter scale, Table 2): Difficult 17.8 (4.3) -> 18.1 (4.2); Medium 16.9 (4.9) -> 17.8 (4.0); Easy 17.0 (4.6) -> 17.6 (4.5); Control 17.5 (4.8) -> 17.5 (4.3). Only Medium's gain differed from zero; no condition effect, F(3,206)=0.99, p=.398, r=.12.
- Math performance, total TTA (Table 2): Difficult 136.0 (32.8) -> 142.2 (33.7); Medium 131.4 (37.9) -> 140.4 (39.5); Easy 137.7 (36.6) -> 149.3 (35.6); Control 143.6 (36.5) -> 144.6 (37.6). Analyses used addition-subtraction only (range 0-100) because multiplication/division hit ceiling: Difficult 68.4 (14.1) -> 71.3 (15.8) [+2.9]; Medium 68.0 (15.5) -> 71.8 (16.3) [+3.8]; Easy 67.7 (16.4) -> 73.9 (15.9) [+6.2]; Control 71.9 (13.4) -> 73.5 (15.4) [+1.6].
- Performance ANOVA on difference scores (Section 3.4): main effect of condition F(3,207)=3.65, p=.018, r=.20; "participants in condition Easy improved more than participants in the control condition, p=.001. Participants in experimental conditions Medium and Difficult did not improve significantly more than participants in the control condition"; gains were significantly >0 for all three experimental conditions but not control. Gender main effect F(1,207)=4.07, p=.045, r=.12 (girls improved more).
- Mediation (Section 3.5, Table 3; Preacher & Hayes 2008 scripts; experimental conditions only, covariates grade and gender): Step 1 total effect of success rate on addition-subtraction gain B=1.416, SE 0.741, beta=.154 (p<.1), R2=.07, F(3,147)=3.39, p=.020. Step 2 adding problems attempted: success rate B=0.968, SE 0.749, beta=.105 (n.s.); problems attempted B=0.004, SE 0.002, beta=.204 (p<.05); delta-R2=.04, delta-F(1,146)=6.29, p=.013. "the indirect effect ... was estimated at -.45, and differed significantly from zero (bootstrapped 95%-confidence interval ranged from -1.50 to -.03). Hence, increasing success rate indirectly increased math improvement". For anxiety (R2=.03, F(3,147)=1.63, p=.185) and competence (R2=.01, F(3,147)=0.59, p=.621) neither the total effect nor the mediation step was significant (delta-F(1,146)=0.25, p=.620 and 1.37, p=.244).
- Discussion caveat: "even in the easy condition, children still solved 19% of the problems incorrectly, so the problems were not overly easy"; benefits "may have been due to increased practice of math" since Math Garden was additional to lessons.
- Klinkenberg, Straatemeier & van der Maas 2011 (Computers & Education 57:1813-1824) abstract: "Using a new item response model based on the Elo (1978) rating system and an explicit scoring rule, estimates of the ability of persons and the difficulty of items are updated with every answered item ... In the scoring rule both accuracy and response time are accounted for. Items are sampled with a mean success probability of .75, making the tasks challenging yet not too difficult. In a period of ten months our sample of 3648 children completed over 3.5 million arithmetic problems. The children completed about 33% of these problems outside school hours." — [CORE record 43750912](https://core.ac.uk/works/43750912) ([DOI 10.1016/j.compedu.2011.02.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2011.02.003)).
- Jansen 2013 adds why >50% is feasible: "In computer-adaptive testing, an expected success rate of 50% is used ... Math Garden allows for higher expected success rates (60% and above) because response time is used as an additional source of information on individuals' ability levels" (p.191).

### Inferences
- Effect on performance is small: the Easy-minus-Control gain is ~4.6 points on a 0-100 scale (6.2 vs 1.6), and only the Easy condition separated from control; the reported total effect of success rate is itself only marginal (p<.1), so the headline "easier = more learning" rests mainly on the mediation path through volume of practice.
- The sign inconsistency in the mediation (indirect effect reported as -.45 with a negative CI while total minus direct = 1.416 - 0.968 = +0.45) is almost certainly a coding/sign-reporting artefact in the paper; the authors' verbal interpretation is a positive indirect effect. Treat the magnitude (~0.45 points per condition step) as the usable number.
- Achieved rates compressed toward the middle (66/72/81 instead of 60/75/90), so the effective manipulation spanned ~15 percentage points, not 30.

### Gaps
- Per-condition dropout is not reported; only total attrition (39 absent + 6 excluded of 252).
- Klinkenberg 2011 full text was not retrievable (CORE: "Not available for public API users"; DARE record links only to the paywalled DOI; no open PDF via Unpaywall/OpenAlex). The exact item-sampling mechanism for the .75 target (e.g., sampling around ability with a fixed difficulty offset, K-factor/uncertainty handling) could not be quoted; only the abstract statement "Items are sampled with a mean success probability of .75" is confirmed.
- Jansen 2013 gives no K factor or "difficulty offset" numbers; it defers to Klinkenberg 2011.

## Q2. Papoušek & Pelánek (AIED 2015), Papoušek, Stanislav & Pelánek (LAK 2016), and the target-success-rate RCT (FI MU tech report 2016 / UMUAI 2017)

### Takeaway
In outlinemaps.org / slepemapy.cz (open geography-facts practice, mostly Czech users, in-school ~20% of data; population is anonymous web users including school students, not age-controlled), the actual 50/65/80/95% target-success RCT (Nov 2015-Jan 2016, ~37,000 learners, ~3.3M answers) found: easier targets win on short-term survival (89.2% -> 81.2% after 10 questions from C95 to C50), harder targets win on return rate (15.2% -> 16.8%) and on learning-curve slope (C95 clearly worst), and self-rated difficulty is best at 65% target (66% "Appropriate" vs 55% for C95). The AIED 2015 paper found the perceived-difficulty peak at ~65% achieved success (75% for in-school users), no significant trend of answers-count with target rate, and a dynamic difficulty adjustment that raised median answers from 21 to 28 (p=0.02).

### Cited Findings
AIED 2015, "Impact of Adaptive Educational System Behaviour on Student Motivation" — [PDF](http://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/aied15.pdf):
- Implementation of the target: item score S_prob(P_est, P_target) = P_est/P_target if P_target >= P_est, else (1-P_est)/(1-P_target); plus S_time(t) = -1/t and S_count(n) = 1/sqrt(1+n); weights W_prob=1, W_count=1, W_time=12 (Section 2.1). Default target 75% (Section 2.2). Multiple-choice option count n = closest integer to 1/G with G = (P_target - P_est)/(1 - P_est), 2 <= n <= 6 (Section 2.3). Dynamic adjustment moves the target based on success on the last 10 questions (Fig. 1; function not given numerically in text).
- Metrics: explicit feedback dialog after 30, 70, 120, 200 answers ("Too Easy"/"Appropriate"/"Too Difficult"); engagement = total answers, median as summary, t-test on log(answers). Median answers by first rating: Too Easy 91, Appropriate 110, Too Difficult 97 (Section 3.2).
- Experiment 1 (adaptive vs random, >30,000 answers), Table 1 median answers per student: adaptive/adaptive 33.0; adaptive/random 20.0; random/adaptive 20.0; random/random 19.5; fully adaptive vs others p<0.01 in two cases, p=0.06 vs random/random (only 26 students in that group).
- Experiment 2 (target probability varied, without dynamic adjustment): "With regard to the total number of answers we have not discovered any statistically significant trend"; "Although the target probability is from the interval [50%, 95%], the average real success rate varies only from 65% to 90%"; perceived difficulty vs achieved success rate is U-shaped with the "Appropriate" peak and the Too Easy = Too Difficult crossover at 65% success (Fig. 2, >1,700,000 answers, 12,000 feedback records); in-school users' optimum ~75% and they are "generally less satisfied"; max satisfaction vs target probability at 60-65% target, "which corresponds to the 70% from the perspective of the real success rate" (Fig. 3).
- Experiment 3 (dynamic adjustment on/off; 176,000 answers, 1,450 feedback records): average success ~75% in both; median answers 28 (enabled) vs 21 (disabled), p=0.02; "Appropriate" ratio 68% vs 63%.
- Conclusion: "a good target success rate is around 65%"; number of answers "did not get significant trend with respect to the target rate".

FI MU technical report FIMU-RS-2016-02, "Evaluation of the Impact of Question Difficulty on Engagement and Learning" (Papoušek, Stanislav, Pelánek, March 2016) — [PDF](https://www.fi.muni.cz/reports/files/2016/FIMU-RS-2016-02.pdf). This is the target-difficulty RCT (conditions expressed as target ERROR rate 5/20/35/50% = target success 95/80/65/50%):
- Design: "four experimental conditions which differ only in one aspect - the target error rate: 5%, 20%, 35%, 50%. Learners were assigned to one of the conditions randomly when they entered the system for the first time ... performed from November 2015 to January 2016, we have collected almost 3 300 000 answers from roughly 37 000 learners" (Section 2.2). Per-condition n not reported (random assignment of ~37,000, so ~9,000 each is implied but not stated). Data set published: fi.muni.cz/adaptivelearning/data/slepemapy/2016-ab-target-difficulty.zip.
- Achieved vs target: "the real error rate is not exactly the same" (noisy behaviour, imperfect model, too few hard items); differences between conditions are largest at the start and "all conditions except C5 are in most contexts really similar after 40 questions" (Section 2.3, Fig. 2). C5 often asks 2-option questions.
- Table 1 (global engagement): survival rate after 10 questions (CI +/-0.77%) C5 89.2%, C20 87.0%, C35 84.0%, C50 81.2%; return rate after >10 h (CI +/-0.75%) C5 15.2%, C20 16.0%, C35 16.6%, C50 16.8%. "Survival rates after 100 answers are very similar in all conditions (from 26.0% to 26.5%, confidence interval +/-0.88%)".
- Explicit feedback (>40,000 ratings, asked after 30 answers): "Appropriate" C35 66% (best), C5 55% (worst), C20 and C50 "only slightly below C35"; "Too easy" C5 35% vs 19-21% for the others; "Too difficult" 10-17%. "Most learners report that question difficulty is appropriate when their achieved error rate is around 30%".
- Learning (reference questions = every 10th question, random open item; power-law fit a*x^-k): "more difficult practice seems to lead to better learning ... the largest differences being between C5 and other conditions"; robust to filtering by minimum answers, >10 h delay, and response-time curves: "C5 is clearly the worst and differences among the others are small" (Section 4.1, Fig. 5). Numeric k values per condition appear only in Fig. 5 (not extractable as text).
- Attrition bias: in C5 (European states) learners who stay longer have lower initial knowledge, which "can be partially caused" the C5 learning-rate deficit (Section 4.2).
- Discussion: "Easy questions lead to better short term engagement, whereas difficult questions are better for the long term engagement ... learning improvement, which is better for more difficult questions (the main difference being between very simple questions and others). These results are in contrast with previous research [Jansen 2013, Lomas 2013]"; design implication "start with easy questions 'to hook learners up' and then switch to more difficult questions".

UMUAI 2017, "Elo-based Learner Modeling for the Adaptive Practice of Facts" (Pelánek, Papoušek, Řihák, Stanislav, Nižnan), Section 6.4.2 — [preprint PDF](http://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/umuai-adaptive-practice.pdf): same experiment labelled C50/C65/C80/C95; "survival rates after 150 answers are similar in all conditions with slightly better results for more difficult questions. The return rate increases with the difficulty of questions, the largest difference being between C95 and other conditions. The subjective rating by learners is best for the C65 conditions"; "the results suggest that more difficult practice leads to better learning, the difference being mainly between C95 and other conditions"; achieved success spans only 65-90% for targets 50-95%; perceived-difficulty optimum 60-70% success (75% in-school). Conclusion: "a suitable success rate is around 65%. This is in contrast to previous similar research (Lomas et al, 2013; Jansen et al, 2013) ... This difference may have been due to different types of knowledge components."

LAK 2016, "Evaluation of an Adaptive Practice System for Learning Geography Facts" — [PDF](http://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/lak16-evaluation.pdf) ([DOI 10.1145/2883851.2883884](https://doi.org/10.1145/2883851.2883884)). NOTE: this paper does NOT vary target success; it fixes target error at 20% and compares adaptive vs random construction (A-A, A-R, R-A, R-R), Aug-Oct 2015, >1,300,000 answers from ~20,000 learners, ~16,000 difficulty ratings:
- Table 1 Weibull fit (k, lambda): A-A 0.762, 6.673; A-R 0.793, 5.849; R-A 0.746, 6.349; R-R 0.751, 5.882 (adaptive stem lowers "infant mortality" k; adaptive options raise length-of-stay lambda).
- Table 2 probability of return (CI +/-0.9%): A-A 15.1%, A-R 13.9%, R-A 14.3%, R-R 13.1% ("relative difference between A-A and R-R condition is 15%").
- Explicit feedback: A-A most "Appropriate"; "Too Easy" in R-R vs A-A: Czech regions 33% vs 26%, European countries 41% vs 24%, European cities 33% vs 24%; Czech mountains 25% vs 23%.
- Learning (reference-question learning curves): "In all cases the conditions with adaptive construction of options (A-A, R-A) beat the conditions with random options (A-R, R-R). The item selection part does not seem to have large effect on learning"; R-A slightly better than A-A. Achieved success (UMUAI recap): A-R and R-R 82%; A-A and R-A 78% (target 75%).
- Population note: "majority of learners is from the Czech Republic (84%) and Slovakia (8%)"; in-school ~20% of data; "80% of answers are within the first session of a learner".

### Inferences
- The geography RCT is the only large randomized test where a harder target (65%) beat an easier one on both learning and long-term engagement; the effect sizes are small in absolute terms (return rate 15.2% vs 16.8%; survival after 100 answers 26.0-26.5% across all conditions) and the dominant contrast is "95% target is bad", not "50% is good".
- Jansen (children, procedural arithmetic, 6-week classroom RCT, external posttest) and Papoušek (open web users, declarative facts, in-system learning curves) disagree in direction; both author groups attribute this to domain/knowledge-component differences, and Papoušek additionally shows attrition bias can inflate the apparent learning of harder conditions.

### Gaps
- Per-condition learner counts and numeric learning-rate k per condition for the 5/20/35/50% experiment are only in figures; not reported as text in the tech report or UMUAI paper.
- AIED 2015 Experiment 2 does not list its exact target-probability conditions or n per condition; it only states the interval [50%, 95%].
- The task's "50/65/80/95%" attribution to LAK 2016 is incorrect; those conditions belong to the FIMU-RS-2016-02 report and UMUAI 2017 Section 6.4.2.

## Q3. Beck & Gong 2013 "Wheel-spinning: students who fail to master a skill" (AIED 2013)

### Takeaway
Mastery = three consecutive first-attempt correct responses without help; wheel-spinning = no mastery within a system-specific checkpoint (10 problems for ASSISTments, 15 for the Cognitive Algebra Tutor). Populations: ASSISTments middle-school math (5,997 students, 45,787 student-skill pairs) and Cognitive Algebra Tutor (575 students, 23,517 pairs); after 10 ASSISTments problems 45% of pairs were unmastered and only 4% more mastered by problem 30 (conditional P(master | not by 10) = 0.089); bounded wheel-spinning prevalence 8-35% (CAT) and 8-45% (ASSISTments) of student-skill pairs. Recommended response: detect early and add "additional tutoring interventions (possibly including human intervention)" rather than more of the same practice.

### Cited Findings
- AIED 2013 abstract (verbatim, from Springer): "We show that if a student does not master a skill in ASSISTments or the Cognitive Tutor quickly, the student is likely to struggle and will probably never master the skill. We discuss connections between such lack of learning and negative student behaviors such as gaming and disengagement, and discuss alterations to ITS design to overcome this issue." — [Springer chapter](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-642-39112-5_44)
- AIED 2013 preview pages (431-432): mastery is "three questions in a row correct" (ASSISTments); a student who has required help on twenty problems has "only a 1.4% chance such a student will ever master the skill". — [Springer preview via reader proxy](https://r.jina.ai/https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-642-39112-5_44)
- The following are from the expanded version of the same study, Chapter 5 "Wheel-Spinning: Student Future Failure in Mastery Learning" of Yue Gong's WPI dissertation (Nov 2014, advisor J. Beck) — [WPI PDF](https://digital.wpi.edu/downloads/9306sz45f):
  - Mastery criterion: "if a student has answered three consecutive problems correctly, we assert him having mastered the skill. To answer a problem correctly, the student has to respond to the problem correctly on his first attempt, and requests no assistance from the tutor prior to that" (Section 4.1).
  - Data after preprocessing: CAT "202,631 algebra problems solved by 575 students ... forming 23,517 student-skill pairs"; ASSISTments "220,539 Math problems solved by 5997 students, forming 45,787 pairs" (Section 4.2).
  - Curve facts (Fig. 9): ~one-third of pairs mastered by the 3rd opportunity ("already known"); 50% mastered after 6 opportunities in both systems; ASSISTments mastery asymptotes ~10th opportunity, CAT ~15th; "The CAT has about 35% student-skill pairs remaining non-mastery at the end, and the number for ASSISTments is about 40%" (treating indeterminate pairs as wheel-spinning).
  - Key ratio: "in ASSISTments, after 10 problems, 45% student-skill instances (= 100%-55%) have not demonstrated mastery ... after practicing 20 more problems, only an additional 4% ... If a student did not demonstrate mastery after 10 problems, then the conditional probability he will master the skill with more practice is 0.04 / 0.45 = 0.089".
  - Checkpoint definition: "If a student who uses the CAT does not master a skill within 15 problems, we categorize him as wheel spinning. For ASSISTments, we set the threshold to 10 problems."
  - Determinate/indeterminate pairs (Table 12): CAT 16,049 (68%) / 7,468 (32%) of 23,517; ASSISTments 28,259 (62%) / 17,528 (38%) of 45,787.
  - Bounded prevalence (Section 5.1): "the wheel-spinning problem could affect 8% - 35% of skill practicing of the CAT students and 8% - 45% of skill practicing of the ASSISTments students. In absolute, the number of possibly wheel-spinning student-skill instances lies in between 1881 and 8230 for the CAT and 3590 and 20,193 for ASSISTment."
  - Time cost (Tables 13-16): CAT 1283 h total, mastery 543 h (42.3%), wheel-spinning 351 h (27.4%), indeterminate 389 h (30.3%), time after the 15-problem checkpoint 166 h (12.9%); bounds 27.4%-57.7% of time on wheel-spinning. ASSISTments 3195 h total, mastery 1454 h (45.5%), wheel-spinning 584 h (18.3%), indeterminate 1157 h (36.2%), time after checkpoint 159 h (5.0%); bounds 18.3%-54.5%.
  - Behaviour of the two groups (Tables 17-18): CAT mastery pairs average 5 problems (4 correct, 1 incorrect), 29 s/problem; wheel-spinning pairs 26 problems (8 correct, 18 incorrect; 30.7% correct), 40 s/problem. ASSISTments mastery 4 problems (3 correct, 1 incorrect; 75% correct), 55 s; wheel-spinning 14 problems (5 correct, 9 incorrect), 59 s.
  - Intervention stance: "wheel spinning is a consequence of a simplified mastery learning framework in many computer tutors, and additional tutoring interventions (possibly including human intervention), should suffice to get the student unstuck in the loop"; early detection "gives the system ample time to do decision making and self-adjustment for better tutoring". A generic logistic-regression detector (features: correct-response count, correct-in-a-row count, exponential-mean response-time z-score, hint-request counts) is trained with 3-fold student-level CV (Section 7).

### Inferences
- The task's shorthand "wheel-spinning = not mastered after 10 opportunities" is the ASSISTments instantiation; the CAT checkpoint is 15. Both are empirical elbows of the mastery curve, not theory-derived.
- Wheel-spinning pairs fail through low accuracy (~31% CAT, ~36% ASSISTments), not near-misses of the 3-in-a-row rule; this matters for any success-rate-targeting design because these students sit far below any 60-85% target.

### Gaps
- The AIED 2013 paper's own full text (10 pages, Springer LNAI 7926 pp.431-440) was not retrievable from any open source; the per-system percentages above come from the 2014 dissertation version (which the chapter states extends the AIED work). Exact numbers printed in the AIED 2013 version (e.g., whether it reported "8-35%/8-45%" or point estimates) could not be verified.
- The "1.4%" figure's exact denominator (twenty problems with help) is from a two-page preview and could not be checked against the full paper.

## Q4. Human tests (2020-2026) of the Wilson et al. 2019 "85% rule"

### Takeaway
Wilson et al. 2019 contains no human data (optimal training accuracy 84.13%, error 15.87%, for gradient-descent binary classifiers). Of ~119 citing works (OpenAlex), three human experiments directly manipulated success/error rate and none supports 85% for humans: Al-Fawakhiri, Kayani & McDougle (bioRxiv 2023, N=194 adults, Pong-like task, 15/30/50/65% error) found peak learning at ~30% error, significantly better than the 15% (85%-success) group; van der Kooij et al. (Sci Rep 2026, N=267, ages 7-58, circle drawing) found 50% target beat 80% target on learning with no motivation difference; Hong et al.-type golf-putting study (RQES 2025, N=150 adults, 60-70% / 20-30% / <10% success) found no difference. No study tested 85% on cognitive/educational (non-motor) human learning.

### Cited Findings
- Wilson, Shenhav, Straccia & Cohen 2019: purely theoretical/simulation (perceptron, 2-layer net on MNIST, Law & Gold monkey model); optimal error rate ER* = 1/2(1 - erf(1/sqrt2)) ~ 0.1587 (accuracy 84.13%) under Gaussian noise; ~81.61% (Laplacian), 75% (Cauchy); scope "binary classification tasks and stochastic gradient-descent-based learning rules"; "no formal theoretical work has addressed the effect of training accuracy on learning, a test of which is an important direction for future work". — [PMC6831579](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6831579/)
- Al-Fawakhiri, Kayani & McDougle, "Evidence of an optimal error rate for motor skill learning" (bioRxiv, 19 Jul 2023): adults 18-35 recruited on Prolific (200 recruited, 16 excluded; N=194 analysed, 46% female, mean age 27+/-5); modified Pong task with paddle size adjusted online to hold a target error rate; four groups at 15%, 30%, 50%, 65% target error; 70 training trials after calibration and a 10-trial baseline. Learning index highest in the 30% group: vs 15% t(89)=3.11, p=0.003, d=0.65; vs 50% t(94)=0.98, p=0.32; vs 65% t(87)=1.58, p=0.12 (Fig. 3C). Binning by achieved error, the sextile centred at 31.66% error had the peak learning index (Fig. 3D). The authors derive ~31.73% as the optimum for continuous signed errors and contrast it with Wilson's ~15% for binary feedback. Abstract: "In a large behavioral dataset (N=192), we observed evidence that learning is greatest at around a ~30% error rate". — [bioRxiv full text](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2023.07.19.549705v1.full); abstract via [OpenAlex](https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.07.19.549705). (N differs between abstract, 192, and text extraction, 194; the text figure is the extractor's reading.)
- van der Kooij, van Mastrigt, van Leeuwen, van der Horst, Burger & Smeets, "Enforcing a high success percentage interferes with reward-based motor learning" (Scientific Reports 2026): N=267 (134 moderate-success, 133 high-success), ages 7-58, recruited at NEMO Science Museum; circle-drawing with unseen hand, binary reward; targets 50% vs 80%; achieved success 49+/-5% vs 72+/-6%. ### Inferences
- Every human manipulation to date is in the motor domain and finds the optimum at 30-50% error (50-70% success), i.e., substantially harder than 85% success; the one study that included an 85%-success arm (Al-Fawakhiri's 15%-error group) found it the worst of four.
- The Wilson rule's derivation assumes binary feedback and gradient descent; the motor studies argue continuous signed errors shift the optimum, so the 85% figure should not be quoted as an empirically validated human target for adaptive practice.

## Q5. Lomas et al. 2013 (CHI) and Lomas et al. 2017 (CHI) — Battleship Numberline

### Takeaway
Lomas, Patel, Forlizzi & Koedinger 2013 (grade 4-8 players on BrainPOP, 10,478 sessions in Exp. 1 and 69,642 in Exp. 2) confirmed: engagement rose monotonically with success rate (quadratic term n.s., p=0.8; "the easier the game, the longer people played"), the largest targets (63% success) yielded 34% more trials and 18% more time than the smallest (29% success), and larger/easier targets learned more slowly per item (target size x learning-rate interaction p=0.02). The 2017 paper's abstract states "even moderate difficulty can reduce player engagement" across >20,000 sessions and shifts to choice, novelty and suspense; its full text was not retrievable.

### Cited Findings
Lomas, Patel, Forlizzi & Koedinger 2013, "Optimizing challenge in an educational game using large-scale design experiments", CHI 2013 pp.89-98 — [PDF (Wayback copy of CMU PACT site)](http://web.archive.org/web/20150614021537/http://pact.cs.cmu.edu/pubs/Lomas,%20Forlizzi%20%26%20Koedinger%202013.pdf) ([DOI 10.1145/2470654.2470668](https://doi.org/10.1145/2470654.2470668)):
- Population: Battleship Numberline on BrainPOP GameUp, "a popular site for classroom teachers in grades 4-8"; anonymous sessions ("the unit of experimental manipulation was on a game session, rather than a subject"), ~1,000 sessions/day.
- Exp. 1: 2x3 between-subjects (target type ship/submarine x error tolerance 3%/5%/10%), 12/16/2011-3/8/2012, 10,478 sessions, 80-trial cap. Table 1 (target size: N, total trials, total time s, accuracy, success rate, reaction time s): 3%: 3462, 12.6 (13.1), 60.9 (54.1), 78.8% (26.5), 29.2% (25.2), 6.8 (5.8); 5%: 3479, 14.6 (14.4), 66.5 (55.5), 79.5% (26.0), 43.3% (30.3), 6.6 (5.6); 10%: 3537, 17.0 (15.8), 71.6 (55.4), 79.6% (25.6), 62.8% (34.1), 6.3 (5.6); all differences p<0.001 except accuracy p=.36. Text: "the largest targets had an average success rate of 63% (sd=34%) while the smallest targets had a success rate of 29% (sd=25%). The larger and easier-to-hit targets were also significantly more engaging, resulting in 34% more estimates and 18% more time spent than the smaller and more difficult-to-hit targets" (17.0/12.6 = +34.9% trials; 71.6/60.9 = +17.6% time).
- Exp. 1 curve shape: "our results show a linear relationship between difficulty and engagement (the quadratic fit was not significant, p=0.8). In short, the easier the game, the longer people played" (Fig. 3); caveat "the average success rate of the easiest conditions were still <70%; therefore, it is possible that we only measured the left side of the Inverted U".
- Exp. 2 ("super experiment"): 2x2x9x8x4x25 design (ability x target type x 9 target sizes 4-40% x 8 time limits 2-30 s x 4 sequencing rules x 25 item sets), 69,642 sessions, 3/25/2012-5/4/2012, 4-item in-game pretest (r=.46 with hit rate, alpha=.62). Engagement = log(time x trials). "Surprisingly, the extremely large targets did not lead to less engagement than moderately sized targets ... the larger the target was, the greater the success rate and the greater the engagement"; longer time limits raised success and engagement; Naive0 immediate repetition raised success but had "a minimal effect on engagement" (only low-ability players more engaged, p=0.024). Predicted-success model R2=.34 (design factors) -> .46 (adding ability); Fig. 6: "low levels of challenge (high success rates) never appear to negatively affect player engagement"; "While the effect of game challenge is slightly curvilinear, it is not U-shaped".
- Learning per item: 1,392 players with random sequencing and >30 decimal trials; error vs log(opportunities) over first 30 trials; "larger targets result in a slower rate of learning, as compared to smaller targets; i.e., target size has a significant interaction with the rate of learning (p=0.02). Therefore, while the largest targets were optimal for engagement, they do not appear to be optimal for learning" (Fig. 7); low-ability students had steeper curves (p=0.002); proportion of low-ability differed across conditions by only 2 percentage points.
- Discussion: "It is possible that, despite our efforts, we never made the game easy enough. The Inverted-U hypothesis could still stand if the theoretically optimal success rate is very high (>90%)"; contrasts with Atkinson (50%), Shapira (25-40%) and Csikszentmihalyi's chess study (~20% success, opponents rated 262 points higher).
- Abstract (independent confirmation): "two large-scale (10K and 70K subjects), multi-factor (2x3 and 2x9x8x4x25) online experiments ... subjects were more engaged and played longer when the game was easier ... the most engaging design conditions produced the slowest rates of learning" — [OpenAlex abstract](https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.1145/2470654.2470668)

Lomas et al. 2017, "Is Difficulty Overrated? The present work investigates other design factors that may account for the purported benefits of difficulty, such as choice, novelty and suspense. ### Inferences
- The "+34% trials / +18% time" numbers are Exp. 1 main effects of target size (10% vs 3% error tolerance), i.e., 63% vs 29% success; they are not a comparison against a moderate optimum, and the whole observed range stayed below ~70% success in Exp. 1.
- "Learning per item" in Lomas 2013 is a slope difference on a learning curve within the first 30 trials, not a posttest; combined with the engagement result it establishes an engagement/learning trade-off rather than a learning benefit of ease.
