# Operationalizing the ZPD as a target success rate / difficulty band

Scope: how adaptive learning systems turn "zone of proximal development" into a number (target P(correct) or difficulty band), what human evidence says about where that number should sit, and what is gained/lost at 85–95% vs 50–70%. Evidence tiers used below: **T1** = randomized/controlled human experiment or large-scale randomized field experiment; **T2** = large observational/log analysis or modeling grounded in data; **T3** = theory, simulation, or vendor/blog claim (flagged). Dates: 2001–2026, with a few older foundational cites.

Research-process note: web-search budget ran out mid-task; several paywalled primaries (Jansen 2013, Beck & Gong 2013 abstract, Ortner 2014 full text) were reached only via secondary quotes or Crossref metadata. Those are flagged in the Gaps of each section.

## Q1. What does the human "optimal difficulty" literature say about the success rate at which learning is fastest and most durable?

### Takeaway
There is no single human-validated "optimal success rate." The 85% rule is a model result for binary gradient-descent learners with explicit caveats that humans were untested; human memory work instead shows (a) learners self-select the *easiest not-yet-known* items and benefit from doing so, (b) *successful-but-effortful* retrieval beats easy retrieval, (c) *unsuccessful* attempts followed by feedback still help, and (d) high-confidence errors are corrected best. Together these point to a moderately high success band for practice (roughly 70–90% depending on phase) with the caveat that in-session performance systematically overstates learning.

### Cited Findings

**85% rule (T3 — model, humans untested)**
- Wilson, Shenhav, Straccia & Cohen (2019, Nature Communications): for gradient-descent binary classifiers with Gaussian noise "the optimal error rate for learning is 15.87%, and the optimal accuracy is around 85%"; demonstrated on a perceptron, a two-layer MNIST network, and the Law & Gold monkey perceptual-learning model. — [PMC6831579](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6831579/)
- Same paper, explicit limits: applies to the *rate* of learning (not retention); binary classification only ("extensions... should consider... more than two categories"); "not all models will exhibit a sweet spot" — "a Bayesian learner (with perfect memory) would learn equally well if hard or easy examples are presented first"; the number is noise-distribution dependent (optimal accuracy 82% for Laplacian, 75% for Cauchy noise); and "to the best of our knowledge no formal theoretical work has addressed the effect of training accuracy on learning, a test of which is an important direction for future work." — [PMC6831579](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6831579/)
- Popular coverage frames it as "learning is optimized when we fail 15% of the time" (ScienceDaily 2019) — this is the model claim, not a human result. — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2019/11/191105113457.htm)

**Region of Proximal Learning — what learners choose and where gains are (T1, lab)**
- Metcalfe & Kornell (2005, JML): RPL model — people decline study for items with high JOLs, then "opt to study the easiest as yet unlearned items" first; they stop an item when their judged rate of learning (jROL) approaches zero; six experiments with Columbia undergraduates support this. Experiment 5 (N=28; 3×3 grid of easy/medium/hard Spanish translations, 45 s): mean difficulty of first three picks ≈1 (easy), next three ≈2 (medium), later picks still well below 3; F(8,26)=90.98, p<.0001; easy and medium items selected more often than hard (F(2,27)=16.40). "When selecting among unknown items, people chose easier items both earlier and more frequently." — [Metcalfe & Kornell 2005 PDF](http://www.columbia.edu/cu/psychology/metcalfe/PDFs/Metcalfe%20Kornell%202005.pdf)
- Kornell & Metcalfe (2006): allowing self-regulated study improved learning; "when choosing among unknown items, participants tended to select easier unknown items and benefited from that choice." — [Kornell & Metcalfe 2006 PDF](https://sites.williams.edu/nk2/files/2011/08/Kornell.Metcalfe.2006a.pdf)
- Metcalfe (Bjork Festschrift chapter): RPL says study "the easiest possible as-yet-unlearned items — just beyond what the learner has already fully mastered, but not much beyond"; cites Nelson & Leonesio's "labor in vain" effect (extra time on the hardest items "results in no memory benefit"); concludes RPL and Desirable Difficulties "agree that this region — a region that is challenging (i.e., desirably difficult) but not too challenging — is the 'just right' level of difficulty." Explicitly links RPL to Vygotsky's ZPD and to "just right" leveled reading. — [Metcalfe chapter PDF](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/psychology/metcalfe/PDFs/Metcalfe-BjorkVolSubmitFeb14Final.pdf)

**Retrieval effort hypothesis — harder successful retrieval, and how many successes (T1, lab)**
- Pyc & Rawson (2009, JML): N=129 Kent State undergraduates, 70 Swahili–English pairs; ISI short vs long (between-S), criterion level 1, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8 or 10 correct retrievals (within-S), retention interval 25 min or 1 week. Final cued recall: short ISI M=.30 vs long ISI M=.54 — longer (harder) spacing between *successful* retrievals roughly doubled retention. Criterion level showed diminishing returns in 3 of 4 groups: a power function fit the criterion→recall curve better than linear (R² 91% vs 74%). (The short-ISI/long-RI group was at floor: 1 vs 10 correct retrievals did not differ.) — [Pyc & Rawson 2009 PDF](https://andymatuschak.org/prompts/Pyc2009.pdf)
- Vaughn & Rawson (2011, Psychological Science): Lithuanian–English pairs learned to criterion of 1–5 correct recalls; higher criterion improves associative memory "although with diminishing returns as the initial learning criterion increased." — [Vaughn & Rawson 2011](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797611417724)

**Unsuccessful retrieval still helps (T1, lab)**
- Kornell, Hays & Bjork (2009, JEP:LMC): Exp 1 (fictional trivia, N=25): test-then-study M=.41 vs read-only M=.31, t(24)=2.97, d=0.58; Exp 3 (weak-associate cue–target pairs): .71 vs .50, t(14)=5.77, d=1.49; retrieval attempts that *failed* (correct guesses excluded) still enhanced learning of the answer once shown; Exp 5 held with a 38 h delay. Conclusion: "taking challenging tests — instead of avoiding errors — may be one key to effective learning." — [Kornell, Hays & Bjork 2009 PDF](https://web.williams.edu/Psychology/Faculty/Kornell/Publications/Kornell.Hays.Bjork.2009.pdf)

**Hypercorrection (T1, lab)**
- Butterfield & Metcalfe (2001): general-knowledge questions with confidence ratings, feedback, retest after 5 min; mean within-participant gamma between confidence-in-error and later correction = +.36 — high-confidence errors are the *most* likely to be corrected; explained as heightened attention to surprising feedback. — [PubMed 11713883](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11713883/)
- Butler, Fazio & Marsh (2011, PB&R): the hypercorrection effect persists over one week, but high-confidence errors also tend to return. — [Butler et al. 2011](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-011-0173-y)
- Metcalfe (2017, Annual Review of Psychology, "Learning from Errors"): review concludes errorful learning followed by corrective feedback is beneficial; hypercorrection replicated across many labs; smaller in older adults. — [Metcalfe 2017](https://www.annualreviews.org/doi/10.1146/annurev-psych-010416-044022)

**Performance ≠ learning (T1/T2 reviews)**
- Soderstrom & Bjork (2015, Perspectives on Psychological Science 10:176–199): performance during acquisition "is often an unreliable index" of learning; "certain manipulations can have opposite effects on learning and performance"; people misread acquisition performance as a guide to long-term learning. — [Bjork Lab](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/publication/soderstrom-n-c-bjork-r-a-2015-learning-versus-performance-an-integrative-review-perspectives-on-psychological-science-10-176-199-doi-10-11771745691615569000/)
- Guadagnoli & Lee (2004, J Motor Behavior) challenge point framework: "increases in functional task difficulty result in decreased performance expectations but in an increase in the available interpretable information" — the "performance–learning paradox"; the optimal challenge point is where learning is maximized while practice-performance detriment is minimized, and it moves up with skill. — [Challenge point (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/8574634_Challenge_Point_A_Framework_for_Conceptualizing_the_Effects_of_Various_Practice_Conditions_in_Motor_Learning); [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Challenge_point_framework)
- Akizuki & Ohashi (2015, Human Movement Science) asked directly "what level of difficulty corresponds to the optimal challenge point?" (motor task). — [ScienceDirect listing](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167945715300105) (abstract not retrieved; see Gaps)
- A 2025 scoping review of the challenge point framework exists (J Motor Behavior). — [Tandfonline](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00222895.2025.2508283) (content not retrieved; see Gaps)

### Inferences
- The "85%" number has no direct human test in the sources found; treat it as a heuristic prior, not evidence. Its own authors say the optimum shifts with the noise model (75–85%) and vanishes for a perfect-memory Bayesian learner.
- The memory literature converges on a two-part rule: pitch *new* practice at the easiest not-yet-known item (RPL), then make each *successful* retrieval as effortful as possible without failing (spacing, delay) — success matters more than difficulty per se, but easy success is nearly worthless (Pyc & Rawson's short-ISI .30).
- Errors are cheap if feedback is immediate and the learner was confident: an error rate of 15–30% in retrieval practice is not a loss when the item is then shown and retested.
- Diminishing returns after ~3 successful retrievals (Pyc & Rawson; Vaughn & Rawson) is the empirical basis for "N correct in a row" mastery criteria (see Q2/Q3).

### Gaps
- No human replication or falsification of the 85% rule was found (search budget exhausted before a targeted query; the 2019 paper itself calls a human test "future work"). Treat any "85% for humans" claim as unsupported.
- Akizuki & Ohashi 2015 numbers (the empirically located optimal challenge point) and the 2025 scoping review's conclusions were not retrievable (paywall).
- Metcalfe & Kornell report which items learners *choose*; the size of the learning gain from studying RPL items vs hardest items is in Kornell & Metcalfe 2006 but exact numbers were not extracted.

## Q2. How do deployed systems pick difficulty / target success, and what does the Math Garden target-success experiment show?

### Takeaway
Assessment engines target P≈0.5 (ALEKS knowledge checks, max-information CAT); practice engines target 0.75 (Math Garden, Outline Maps), 0.62 (PISA highly adaptive test, chosen to protect motivation), or "ready to learn" fringe items that succeed ~80–86% of the time (ALEKS learning mode); mastery gates use 0.95 (BKT) or "3 correct in a row"/100% on an exercise (Khan). The key human experiment (Jansen et al. 2013, Math Garden 60/75/90%) shows the success-rate manipulation moved *how much children practiced*, and performance gains were mediated by number of problems attempted, not by direct effect of difficulty; anxiety improved equally in all conditions.

### Cited Findings

**Math Garden / Elo practice (T1 field RCT + T2 log data)**
- Klinkenberg, Straatemeier & van der Maas (2011, Computers & Education 57:1813–1824): Elo-based adaptive practice with response-time scoring; "items are sampled with a mean success probability of .75, making the tasks challenging yet not too difficult"; 3,648 children, >3.5 million problems over 10 months; ~33% of problems done outside school hours; "better measurement precision, high validity and reliability, high pupil satisfaction." — [ERIC EJ925823](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ925823)
- Jansen, Louwerse, Straatemeier, van der Ven, Klinkenberg & van der Maas (2013, Learning and Individual Differences 24:190–197): manipulated preset success rate at 60%, 75%, 90% in Math Garden to test whether children "independent of ability level" would show less anxiety and higher perceived competence at high success. Secondary reports: "Mathematics anxiety scores improved equally in all conditions" — [ERIC EJ1110268 (Alleviating math anxiety, citing Jansen)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1110268.pdf); "performance improvement in the program Math Garden was mediated by the number of tasks attempted" and the authors "did not find any effects" of practice on perceived competence or anxiety — [Bernecker et al. 2023, J. Intelligence / PMC10299571](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10299571). Search-engine summary of the abstract: "Selection of problems depends on the estimation of an individual's ability level and on a preset level of expected success rate." — [ScienceDirect listing](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1041608012001951)
- Bernecker et al. (2023): 376 fifth graders (200 experimental / 170 control, ~20.7 weeks) on standard Math Garden ("average percentage of correct tasks of 75%"): math self-concept rose in the experimental group (β=.12, p=.002, d=0.26); more practiced tasks predicted better subtraction performance (β=.10, p=.008); no addition effect; no anxiety effect. — [PMC10299571](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10299571)
- Pelánek (2016, Computers & Education, Elo review): the Outline Maps geography system "uses a target success rate (e.g., 75%) and adaptively selects questions in such a way that students' achieved performance is close to this target"; Math Garden and MatMat use Elo extensions with response time. — [Pelánek 2016 preprint](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S036013151630080X)

**ALEKS (T2 vendor research, large logs)**
- Assessment: the next question is chosen so that "the probabilities of the states that contain it add to about 0.5... the item has a likelihood close to 0.5"; observed proportion of assessment items actually known "is somewhere around 0.5, which is the desired value." — [Matayoshi et al. 2021 preprint (JMP)](https://jmatayoshi.github.io/publications/JMP2021_KST_ALEKS_preprint.pdf)
- Learning mode: the student picks from the *outer fringe* ("what the student is ready to learn"); learning-attempt outcomes over tens of millions of attempts: success rate 0.809 (Sixth-Grade Math, N=38.9M) and 0.841 (Algebra I, N=21.8M); across the 500 most-used items, "92.4% of the items have a success rate of 0.70 or higher, and the median success rate is 0.863"; 14 items fell below 0.60 and are treated as diagnostic of a wrong fringe placement. — [Matayoshi et al. 2021 preprint](https://jmatayoshi.github.io/publications/JMP2021_KST_ALEKS_preprint.pdf)
- Falmagne et al. (ALEKS science paper): assessment picks the problem with "about a 50% chance of knowing"; stops on an entropy criterion; "learning proceeds by mastering a new problem in the outer fringe." — [Science Behind ALEKS PDF](https://www.aleks.com/about_aleks/Science_Behind_ALEKS.pdf)
- Search-summary claim (not verified in primary): median estimated probability of successfully learning an outer-fringe item ≈ .93 for elementary math. — [ALEKS validity paper](https://www.aleks.com/paper_psych/Validity_in_L_Spaces.pdf) (flag: vendor, number from a search summary)

**Cognitive Tutor / MATHia (T2)**
- BKT mastery threshold: "until the student has 'mastered' the rule (typically defined as a 0.95 probability of knowing the rule)." — [NAP: Cognitive Tutor Algebra I](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/read/10297/chapter/6)
- Problem selection: "among all the task categories involving unmastered knowledge components, the algorithm picks the one that is estimated to be closest to mastery (i.e., closest to 0.95 without being over)" — a "gentle slope." — [Tutor Modeling vs Student Modeling (AAAI)](https://cdn.aaai.org/ocs/4483/4483-21568-1-PB.pdf)
- Doroudi et al. note BKT mastery learning implicitly optimizes a reward of 1 when P(known) exceeds 0.95, and that the optimal policy under this reward "can be very different" from one rewarding actual latent learning. — [Doroudi et al. 2019 author version](https://faculty.sites.uci.edu/doroudis/files/2019/11/IJAIED-RL-Review-Author-Version.pdf)

**Khan Academy (T3 vendor documentation)**
- Levels: Familiar = 70–85% on an exercise (or all correct on a quiz/test); Proficient = 100% of a skill's questions in an exercise/quiz/test; Mastered = the proficient skill answered correctly on the Unit Test. Points: Familiar 50, Proficient 80, Mastered 100. — [Khan Academy Help Center](https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/en-us/articles/5548760867853--How-do-Khan-Academy-s-Mastery-levels-work)

**Duolingo (T3 vendor + press)**
- Birdbrain predicts "how likely the learner is to get that exercise correct," aiming to keep learners "in the zone where they remain engaged but are still learning at the edge of their abilities"; "when students are given material that's too difficult, they often get frustrated and quit"; ~20% of lessons are not completed; A/B tests "consistently caused both engagement and learning measures to increase" (Birdbrain V1 and V2). No numeric target published. — [IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/duolingo)
- Half-life regression (Settles & Meeder 2016, ACL): a spaced-repetition *scheduling* model fitted on 13M learning traces; it is not a difficulty-targeting rule. — [ACL P16-1174](https://aclanthology.org/anthology-files/pdf/P/P16/P16-1174.pdf)
- The widely repeated claim that Duolingo targets a "50% Goldilocks zone" comes from third-party blogs, not Duolingo. — [Medium (third-party)](https://medium.com/@rohithparambil/how-duolingo-predicts-when-youll-forget-using-data-mining-2abab0a921f4) (flag: unverified vendor-adjacent claim)

**CAT: 50% vs success-constrained selection (T2 psychometrics + T1 motivation study)**
- Eggen & Verschoor (2006, Applied Psychological Measurement 30:379–393): "maximum information item selection in CATs... results in each individual answering about 50% of the items correctly"; presents two selection procedures for easier tests; "an alternative selection procedure, based on maximum information at a shifted ability level, gives satisfactory results with both [1PL and 2PL] models." — [Crossref abstract](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/0146621606288890). Search-engine summary adds "precision in adaptive testing is still considerably high at success rates of 60% or 70%" (secondary; not verified in full text).
- PISA highly adaptive testing (Frey et al. 2024, Frontiers in Psychology): "We suggest using a response probability (RP) of .62 for a correct answer when calculating the maximum Fisher information during HAT item selection," citing Eggen & Verschoor, expected "to reduce negative test taker emotions such as anxiety and anger." — [Frontiers 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1446799/full)
- Ortner, Weißkopf & Koch (2014, EJPA): 174 German secondary students (15–21) randomized to CAT vs fixed-item matrices test; "More situational fear of failure and less subjective probability of success were reported using CAT compared to FIT"; flow did not differ; "average reported motivation was lower during CAT." — [Crossref abstract](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1027/1015-5759/a000168)

### Inferences
- The industry split is consistent: **probe/measure at ~0.5, practice at ~0.75–0.85, gate mastery at ≥0.95 or N-in-a-row.** ALEKS's own learning-mode data show what "ready to learn" looks like empirically: ~81–84% first-attempt learning success, item median 0.86.
- Jansen 2013 is the only human randomized test of a target success rate found; its mechanism finding (gains mediated by volume of practice) means a higher target that increases engagement can raise learning *through dose*, even if per-item learning is lower — the same tension Lomas found in games (Q3).
- CAT literature quantifies the cost of raising success from 0.5 to ~0.6–0.7: modest information loss, measurable motivation/affect gain (Ortner; Frey). 0.62 is a defensible probe target when the probe is also a learning experience.

### Gaps
- Jansen 2013 exact per-condition numbers (problems attempted, learning gain, dropout by 60/75/90%) were not retrievable (Elsevier/ResearchGate 403; Crossref and Semantic Scholar abstracts elided). Only the mediation result and the null anxiety/competence result are sourced. Which condition attempted the most problems is NOT sourced here — do not state it without the primary.
- Papoušek & Pelánek (2015, AIED) "Impact of adaptive educational system behaviour on student motivation" — the Outline Maps experiment on adaptive vs non-adaptive selection and target success — could not be downloaded; no numbers.
- Knewton: no primary source retrieved; omitted.
- Duolingo's internal target probability (if any) is unpublished.

## Q3. What do EDM/AIED sequencing policies and large-scale game experiments say about difficulty, engagement, wheel-spinning and stopping?

### Takeaway
Large randomized game experiments (Lomas 2013/2017, ~90k sessions) find engagement rises monotonically with success rate up to ~70% (no inverted U), while the easiest conditions learn slowest per item — so difficulty trades dose against per-item gain. Mastery-learning logs show two failure modes at once: over-practice (58% of practices after mastery; removing it saved 12% of time with no loss) and wheel-spinning (~25% of student-skill pairs in Cognitive Tutor; ~38% of ASSISTments students unmastered after 10 opportunities), detectable with ~0.68 AUC (multi-feature) or >90% precision from correctness features after 4 steps. RL sequencing beats baselines in just over half of studies, mostly when constrained by psychological models.

### Cited Findings

**Lomas et al. — Battleship Numberline (T1, large randomized field experiments)**
- CHI 2013 Experiment 1 (10,478 sessions, Dec 2011–Mar 2012, grades 4–6 estimation game): largest targets → 63% success (SD 34%) vs smallest → 29% (SD 25%); easier targets produced 34% more estimates and 18% more time; accuracy per estimate did not differ (p=.34). Challenge (observed success rate) vs engagement was *linear* ("the quadratic fit was not significant, p=0.8"); caveat: "the average success rate of the easiest conditions were still <70%." — [Lomas, Patel, Forlizzi & Koedinger 2013 PDF](https://pact.cs.cmu.edu/pubs/Lomas,%20Forlizzi%20&%20Koedinger%202013.pdf)
- CHI 2013 Experiment 2 (~70,000 subjects; 2×2×9×8×4×25 design with time limits 2–30 s, 9 target sizes, 25 item sets, 4 sequencing rules, pretest covariate): "in all cases, greater success is associated with greater engagement"; shortest time limits "were the most disengaging of any design factor"; predicted-success-rate vs engagement "slightly curvilinear... not U-shaped — the model does not predict a point where low challenge reduces player motivation." — [same PDF](https://pact.cs.cmu.edu/pubs/Lomas,%20Forlizzi%20&%20Koedinger%202013.pdf)
- Learning curves (n=1,392 players with >30 trials, decimal domain): "larger targets result in a slower rate of learning... (p=0.02). Therefore, while the largest targets were optimal for engagement, they do not appear to be optimal for learning." Low-ability players had steeper curves (p=0.002). — [same PDF](https://pact.cs.cmu.edu/pubs/Lomas,%20Forlizzi%20&%20Koedinger%202013.pdf); abstract: "the most engaging design conditions produced the slowest rates of learning." — [Semantic Scholar record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1145/2470654.2470668?fields=title,abstract,year)
- CHI 2017 "Is difficulty overrated?" (Exp 1 N=10,472; Exp 2 N=5,065; Exp 3 N=6,511; >20,000 sessions): "Moderately difficult levels were most motivating when self-selected; yet, when difficulty was blindly assigned, the easiest games were most motivating"; moderate novelty and "close game" suspense increased motivation; recommendation: make games "not too hard, not too boring." — [TU Delft record](https://research.tudelft.nl/en/publications/is-difficulty-overrated-the-effects-of-choice-novelty-and-suspens)
- Pre-registered replication-style test (Royal Society Open Science 2023; N=311 Prolific adults; AI opponent tuned to win margins +15/0/−15): no difference in enjoyment (F(2,308)=1.29, p=.277) or replay choice (χ²=3.40, p=.183) across difficulty-skill balance conditions. — [PMC9890114](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9890114/)

**Over-practice (T1 classroom experiment + T2 logs)**
- Cen, Koedinger & Junker (2007, AIED): in Cognitive Tutor geometry logs "58% out of 4102 practices and 31% of 636 exercise questions were done after the students had reached mastery"; one over-practiced KC went from 12% to 8% error over 18 practices. Classroom experiment (94 pretested; 73 with valid pre/post): optimized tutor saved 30% of tutoring time in the parallelogram unit, 14% in squares, ~22 minutes total (12% of tutoring time) with no post-test (p=.772) or 2-week retention (p=.602) difference. Reported range across units 10–30%. — [Cen & Koedinger AIED07 PDF](http://pact.cs.cmu.edu/koedinger/pubs/Cen%20&%20Koedinger%20AIED07.pdf)

**Wheel-spinning (T2 logs)**
- Beck & Gong (2013, AIED) operationalized wheel-spinning as "failing to get three attempts correct in a row within the first 10 practice opportunities." — [Zhang et al., EDM 2019 (ERIC ED594575)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED594575.pdf)
- Prevalence: for Cognitive Tutor "the wheel-spinning problem is estimated to affect approximately 25% of student-skill pairs" (Gong & Beck, quoted); MATHia developers treat KC rates >20% as a red flag; ~11.2% of 424,000 completions in a MATHia dataset. — [Towards Practical Detection of Unproductive Struggle, AIED 2020 (ERIC ED606472)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED606472.pdf)
- ASSISTments: "approximately 38% of students did not master a given skill after 10 practice opportunities" (Beck & Gong 2013 as summarized); Kai et al. (JEDM 2018): under Beck & Gong's definition 12.3% of 287,093 student–problem-set pairs would be wheel-spinning, but "many of these students do indeed eventually get three correct in a row and are then able to pass a retention test"; of non-persistent pairs 56.2% mastered before problem 10 and 43.8% quit unmastered; decision-tree detector AUC 0.684 (student-skill CV), 0.636 (student-level CV). — [Kai et al. JEDM](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/download/210/88)
- Zhang, Baker et al. (EDM 2019): wheel-spinning frequency under the 3-in-a-row criterion = 6.6% (CL1), 0.56% (CL2), 10.2% (Geometry) of (student, KC) pairs; under Käser's Predictive-Stability++ criterion = 24.2%, 2.17%, 13.2%; the two criteria agree on wheel-spinning "generally less than 50%" of the time (22.2% / 14.1% / 41.6%). A single-feature logistic regression on "Correct Response Percentage" reached 93.5% precision / 77.1% recall after 4 steps; the Shapley-explainable detector points to "possible gaps in prerequisites" as an actionable feature. — [ERIC ED594575](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED594575.pdf)
- Käser, Klingler, Schwing & Gross (2017, IEEE TLT, DBN student modeling): the Predictive Stability policy stops instruction on a skill "when the predicted probabilities do not" change further; an effort E>10 without mastery "can be considered to be wheel-spinning." — [Käser et al. 2017](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/7837655) ; the LAK 2016 "When to stop? Towards universal instructional policies" paper is the origin. — [ETH Research Collection](https://www.research-collection.ethz.ch/handle/20.500.11850/569191?show=full)
- Bayesian Adaptive Mastery Assessment (2021) proposes personalized stopping rules to "avoid the issue of over-practicing and under-practicing," with shorter assessment for all students in simulation. — [arXiv 2103.03766](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.03766)

**RL / POMDP sequencing (T2 review; T1 within cited studies)**
- Doroudi, Aleven & Brunskill (2019, IJAIED 29:568–620): of the empirical comparisons, "over half of the studies found that RL-induced policies significantly outperform baselines"; four mixed; "Ten studies found no significant difference"; "Only one study found a baseline policy outperformed an RL-induced policy"; successful studies "often found a Cohen's d effect size of at least 0.8"; in the paired-associate/psychological-model cluster "11 out of 14 studies" favored RL policies; RL "has been most successful in cases where it has been constrained with ideas and theories from cognitive psychology." — [Author version PDF](https://faculty.sites.uci.edu/doroudis/files/2019/11/IJAIED-RL-Review-Author-Version.pdf)
- Rafferty, Brunskill, Griffiths & Shafto (2016, Cognitive Science 40(6)): teaching formulated as POMDP planning; first demonstration with human learners that POMDP policies learn faster than random and than maximize-expected-information-gain baselines in some scenarios. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/cogs.12290)

**ZPD-band recommendation with knowledge tracing (T1 field trial)**
- E-Gotsky (2019 preprint; Israeli schools, 4 schools, adaptive vs baseline sections): predicts Correct-at-First-Attempt and Time-To-Success; skips exercises whose CFA is above a threshold AND TTS below a threshold as "below the ZPD," and skips bonus items predicted too hard. Result: adaptive students' first-attempt success was significantly lower (medians 0.773–0.795 vs 0.779–0.874 baseline; corrected p=0.010) — i.e., the policy pushed learners from ~85% toward ~78% success; guessing fell ~25%; quiz effects mixed (improvement-rate p=0.06 favoring adaptive; raw quiz scores favored baseline in one school). — [arXiv 1904.12268](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.12268)
- Generic KT-based recommenders "compute an expected score for each exercise that the student should get to achieve mastery, and... return the exercise with a predicted score that is closest to the expected score," recommending harder items as knowledge state grows. — [Exercise recommendation based on knowledge concept prediction (KBS 2020)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0950705120306109)

### Inferences
- In free-choice/game settings, difficulty is an engagement tax with no observed lower bound down to ~70% success; the only argument for harder items is per-item learning rate, which Lomas measured as real but small relative to the dose effect. For a tutor with captive sessions the calculus shifts toward per-item learning; for voluntary use it shifts toward dose.
- The choice effect (moderate difficulty most motivating when self-selected) is a cheap lever: let the learner opt into "harder" rather than assigning it.
- Wheel-spinning detection is practical after 4–10 attempts using correctness features; the operational rule "3 correct in a row within 10" is crude (criteria disagree >50% of the time) — prefer a model-based stability rule plus a hard cap (E>10) and route to prerequisite repair.
- Over-practice waste is large (≈half of practices after mastery) and removable without learning loss; conversely the BKT 0.95 gate with "closest-to-mastery" selection is itself a high-success policy (it favors nearly-mastered KCs).

### Gaps
- Beck & Gong 2013 primary abstract unavailable (Springer/Crossref elided); prevalence numbers are via Kai et al. 2018 and the AIED 2020 paper.
- No paper found that empirically compares P(correct) bands (e.g., 0.6–0.9) for KT-based recommendation with learning outcomes; E-Gotsky is the closest (threshold-based, mixed results). Search budget ended before a targeted query.
- Lomas 2017 learning outcomes not reported in the record fetched.

## Q4. What are the explicit trade-offs, and how should the target vary by phase, learner, and format?

### Takeaway
Four dials conflict: information (max at P=0.5), motivation/dose (rises to ≥0.7 with no observed ceiling in free-choice settings), per-item learning (favors effortful success, i.e., lower P and longer spacing), and durability (higher criterion → diminishing returns; performance during practice overstates retention). The defensible resolution is phase-specific targets rather than one number.

### Cited Findings
- Information vs success: max-information CAT ⇒ ~50% correct; shifted-ability selection keeps precision acceptable at higher success — Eggen & Verschoor 2006 [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/0146621606288890); PISA HAT adopts RP=.62 explicitly to reduce anxiety/anger — [Frontiers 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1446799/full); CAT at ~50% raised fear of failure and lowered subjective success probability vs fixed tests (N=174) — Ortner et al. 2014 [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1027/1015-5759/a000168).
- Motivation/dose: easier → more trials and time, linear to ~70% success (Lomas 2013; 10,478 + ~70,000 sessions) — [PDF](https://pact.cs.cmu.edu/pubs/Lomas,%20Forlizzi%20&%20Koedinger%202013.pdf); Math Garden gains mediated by number of tasks attempted (Jansen 2013 via [PMC10299571](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10299571)).
- Acquisition speed vs retention: successful retrieval after long ISI .54 vs short ISI .30 on final test (Pyc & Rawson 2009) — [PDF](https://andymatuschak.org/prompts/Pyc2009.pdf); "certain manipulations can have opposite effects on learning and performance" (Soderstrom & Bjork 2015) — [Bjork Lab](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/publication/soderstrom-n-c-bjork-r-a-2015-learning-versus-performance-an-integrative-review-perspectives-on-psychological-science-10-176-199-doi-10-11771745691615569000/); Guadagnoli & Lee: harder practice lowers practice performance but raises "interpretable information" for learning — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/8574634_Challenge_Point_A_Framework_for_Conceptualizing_the_Effects_of_Various_Practice_Conditions_in_Motor_Learning).
- Diminishing returns on criterion: power-function fit, 91% vs 74% variance; 1 vs 10 successes indistinguishable when performance was at floor (Pyc & Rawson 2009) — [PDF](https://andymatuschak.org/prompts/Pyc2009.pdf); criterion 1–5 diminishing (Vaughn & Rawson 2011) — [SAGE](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797611417724).
- Dropout/wheel-spinning: ~25% of Cognitive Tutor student-skill pairs; 43.8% of non-persistent ASSISTments pairs quit unmastered before problem 10 — [ERIC ED606472](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED606472.pdf); [Kai et al. JEDM](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/download/210/88).
- Guessing floor / format: E-Gotsky used a 2.1 s response-time floor to flag guesses and cut guessing 25% by removing too-easy items — [arXiv 1904.12268](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.12268); Math Garden and MatMat score speed+accuracy because "response time... is an important indicator of knowledge in the case of basic arithmetic" — [Pelánek 2016](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S036013151630080X); Outline Maps uses open questions and 2–6-option MCQ, whose chance floors differ (50%→17%) — [Pelánek 2016](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S036013151630080X).
- Learner level: Lomas — low-ability players learned faster (p=0.002) and were the only group for whom immediate-repeat sequencing raised engagement — [PDF](https://pact.cs.cmu.edu/pubs/Lomas,%20Forlizzi%20&%20Koedinger%202013.pdf); Doroudi — RL policies sometimes help only low performers (aptitude-treatment interaction) — [PDF](https://faculty.sites.uci.edu/doroudis/files/2019/11/IJAIED-RL-Review-Author-Version.pdf); Ortner — CAT's motivational cost falls on higher performers' subjective success — [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1027/1015-5759/a000168); RPL predicts the region shifts to harder items as learning progresses — [Metcalfe & Kornell 2005](http://www.columbia.edu/cu/psychology/metcalfe/PDFs/Metcalfe%20Kornell%202005.pdf).
- Effort/latency as a second dial: Pyc & Rawson used first-keypress latency as the retrieval-difficulty measure (Exp 2); ALEKS learning success by item is used to audit fringe placement (items <0.60 flagged) — [Matayoshi 2021](https://jmatayoshi.github.io/publications/JMP2021_KST_ALEKS_preprint.pdf); Wilson et al.'s model assumes difficulty can be titrated continuously each trial (staircase) — [PMC6831579](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6831579/).

### Inferences (phase table — synthesis, not a single cited source)
- **Probe / diagnosis**: P≈0.5–0.62. Max information at 0.5; 0.62 buys motivation at small precision cost (Eggen & Verschoor; PISA HAT). Use only when the learner knows it's a placement, and keep it short (ALEKS uses an entropy stop).
- **New-skill practice**: target ≈0.75–0.85 first-attempt success on the easiest not-yet-known item (RPL; Math Garden .75; ALEKS fringe ~.81–.86 observed). Below ~0.7 expect dose loss (Lomas); above ~0.9, *hypothesis*, per-item learning falls off. The 0.9 cutoff is unvalidated: Cen's over-practice result concerns practice continued after BKT mastery, and Pyc & Rawson show easier successful retrievals (short ISI) give lower final recall (.30 vs .54), but neither tests a success-rate boundary.
- **Retrieval/review**: schedule so that predicted recall is ~0.85–0.9 at review time (FSRS default 0.9; see Q5) but make each success effortful (spacing, no cue) — retention tracks difficulty-of-success, not count-of-successes after ~3.
- **Mastery gate / exam prep**: require 0.95 BKT or 3-in-a-row on unassisted items; Khan requires 100% on an exercise then a unit-test hit. Stop on stability rather than count to avoid over-practice; cap effort (E>10) to catch wheel-spinning.
- **Novices**: keep P higher and sequence tighter (they gain most per item and disengage fastest); **advanced**: allow self-selected harder items (choice makes moderate difficulty motivating, Lomas 2017).
- **MC formats**: correct the target for chance (a 4-option MC at 0.75 observed ≈ 0.67 true knowledge); use latency floors to discount guesses.

### Gaps
- No study found that directly randomizes learners across target bands and measures *both* retention and dropout in a tutoring (non-game, non-arithmetic) domain; Jansen 2013 is arithmetic drill with children and its full numbers are missing here.
- No sourced number for the optimal MC chance-correction or latency threshold outside E-Gotsky's 2.1 s.

## Q5. Which levers raise success without leaving the ZPD (keep the task hard, raise the odds), and what is the evidence + performance-vs-learning caveat for each?

### Takeaway
Each lever converts "too hard" into "hard but doable": scaffolding/fading, subgoal splitting, prerequisite repair, hint-on-demand, retrieval warm-ups (pretesting), confidence prompts with feedback, and spacing to ~0.9 recall. Evidence is strongest for fading and pretesting; the shared caveat is that levers which make practice smoother (proactive hints, massed review, too-easy fringe items) raise in-session performance more than learning.

### Cited Findings
- **Worked-example fading (T1)**: Renkl, Atkinson & Maier (2000/2002, JEE 70:293–315): faded examples (progressively removing worked steps) outperform example–problem pairs on transfer; "individuals learned most about those principles that were faded"; position of faded step did not matter (Renkl & Atkinson 2003 cognitive-load account). — [Renkl & Atkinson 2003](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/B:TRUC.0000021815.74806.f6); [Renkl, Atkinson & Maier 2002](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/200772684_Transitioning_From_Studying_Examples_to_Solving_Problems_Effects_of_Self-Explanation_Prompts_and_Fading_Worked-Out_Steps). Caveat: expertise-reversal — worked examples help novices and can hurt advanced learners (Kalyuga et al. 2003, cited by Doroudi 2019 as a sequencing principle) — [Doroudi PDF](https://faculty.sites.uci.edu/doroudis/files/2019/11/IJAIED-RL-Review-Author-Version.pdf).
- **Hint-on-demand vs hint-on-error (T1 classroom)**: Razzaq & Heffernan (2010, ITS): "students learned reliably more with hints-on-demand than proactive hints," especially students who request many hints; no difference for low-hint users. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-642-13388-6_39). Assistance dilemma framing (help abuse vs help avoidance) — Aleven & Roll — [Roll et al. 2011](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0959475210000538). Caveat: proactive hints raise immediate correctness while reducing learning for hint-heavy students.
- **Prerequisite repair as the wheel-spinning intervention (T2)**: Shapley-explained detectors surface "possible gaps in prerequisites" as the actionable feature; ALEKS advises "reviewing previous" material when a fringe item fails. — [ERIC ED594575](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED594575.pdf); [Science Behind ALEKS](https://www.aleks.com/about_aleks/Science_Behind_ALEKS.pdf).
- **Retrieval warm-up / pretesting (T1)**: unsuccessful retrieval before study improved later recall (d=0.58 to 1.49) — [Kornell, Hays & Bjork 2009](https://web.williams.edu/Psychology/Faculty/Kornell/Publications/Kornell.Hays.Bjork.2009.pdf). Caveat: benefit depends on immediate feedback; in Exp 2, when read-only time was matched generously, the advantage disappeared (.32 vs .32).
- **Confidence prompts + feedback (T1)**: high-confidence errors are hypercorrected (γ=.36) — [Butterfield & Metcalfe 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11713883/); effect persists a week but errors can return — [Butler, Fazio & Marsh 2011](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-011-0173-y). Lever: ask for confidence before revealing, target feedback to confident errors.
- **Spacing/scheduling to ~R=0.9 (T2/T3 engineering)**: FSRS: "If desired retention is 0.9... the probability that you will recall a card will always oscillate between 100% and 90%"; the wiki defines an optimum that minimizes workload per remembered item, with a workload curve that has a minimum — [fsrs4anki tutorial](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki/blob/main/docs/tutorial.md); [The optimal retention wiki](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki/wiki/The-optimal-retention); community guidance: 80–95% reasonable, above ~95% workload rises steeply — [Expertium blog](https://expertium.github.io/Retention.html). Caveat: retention targets are per-review success rates for *review*, not for new learning; Pyc & Rawson show that the harder (longer-spaced) success is the one that pays.
- **Subgoal splitting**: no primary retrieved (search budget); see Gaps.
- **Easier-but-effortful redesign**: Lomas 2013 sequencing rule "Naïve0" (repeat a missed item immediately) raised success significantly but "had a minimal effect on engagement" except for low-ability players — a reminder that not every success-raising lever buys motivation. — [PDF](https://pact.cs.cmu.edu/pubs/Lomas,%20Forlizzi%20&%20Koedinger%202013.pdf)
- **Self-selection of difficulty**: moderate difficulty was most motivating only when chosen (Lomas 2017) — [TU Delft](https://research.tudelft.nl/en/publications/is-difficulty-overrated-the-effects-of-choice-novelty-and-suspens).

### Inferences
- The levers split into two classes: those that raise success by *reducing the task* (proactive hints, worked steps for experts, massed review) — which mostly raise performance — and those that raise success by *raising the learner* (prerequisite repair, pretesting, faded examples for novices, confidence-targeted feedback, spaced review at ~0.9) — which raise learning. A tutor should prefer the second class and count first-class assists as a cost signal.
- For a step-loop tutor: attempt first (pretest effect), hint only on request or after a stability-detected stall, fade support across attempts, and flag confident errors for explicit correction.

### Gaps
- Subgoal-learning evidence (Catrambone; Margulieux) not retrieved.
- Hint-on-demand effect sizes (Razzaq & Heffernan) not extracted; abstract elided in Crossref.
- No study found that measures whether scaffolding that *raises* first-attempt success from ~0.6 to ~0.8 preserves the per-item learning of the harder version — the central empirical question for "raise success without leaving the ZPD" remains untested in the sources reached.
