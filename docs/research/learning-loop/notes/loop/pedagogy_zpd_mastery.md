# Pedagogy for an adaptive AI tutor's teaching loop: ZPD, mastery, CAT, difficulty, feedback, misconceptions

Scope note. Sources are peer-reviewed where possible; secondary sources (reviews, summaries) are marked as such. "d" = Cohen's d, "g" = Hedges' g. Effect sizes are relative to the study's own control unless stated. Evidence tiers used throughout: **[classroom/RCT-proven]**, **[lab-proven]**, **[theory/model]**, **[extrapolation to LLM tutor]**. Where a primary paper was paywalled, the note names the secondary source actually read.

## KQ1. What effect sizes do meta-analyses give for mastery learning, tutoring/ITS, feedback, retrieval practice, self-explanation, learning-by-teaching, and Socratic/dialogic tutoring — and how much is the 2-sigma claim overstated?

### Takeaway
Bloom's d = 2.0 is an outlier produced by small dissertation studies that held tutees to a higher mastery bar than controls; the robust modern numbers are ~0.5 for mastery learning (dropping to ~0.1 on standardized tests), ~0.8 for both human tutoring and step-based ITS (VanLehn 2011), ~0.5 for feedback and retrieval practice, ~0.55 for self-explanation, ~0.35–0.56 for learning-by-teaching, and ~0.36 for productive failure. Nothing replicates at 2 SD.

### Cited Findings
- Bloom (1984) reported that the average student tutored one-to-one with mastery learning performed two standard deviations above conventional classroom students — [Wikipedia summary of Bloom 1984](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloom's_2_sigma_problem). Secondary source; the primary is Bloom, Educational Researcher 13(6), 1984.
- The 2-sigma studies held tutored students to a 90% mastery criterion while classroom students needed only 80%, and controls advanced regardless of performance; the reviewer concludes "Bloom's d=2.0 effect size seems to be due mostly to holding tutees to a higher standard of mastery" — [Nintil systematic review of the 2-sigma literature](https://nintil.com/bloom-sigma/). Secondary source (well-cited blog review, not peer-reviewed).
- "The 2 sigma effect has never been replicated at that scale" — [Growth Engineering summary](https://www.growthengineering.co.uk/2-sigma-problem/) (secondary, non-academic); consistent with the [Education Next review "Two-Sigma Tutoring: Separating Science Fiction from Science Fact"](https://www.educationnext.org/two-sigma-tutoring-separating-science-fiction-from-science-fact/).
- Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns (1990), meta-analysis of 108 controlled evaluations: mastery learning has positive effects on exam performance in college, high school and upper elementary; effects stronger on weaker students; vary with mastery procedure, design and content — [Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns 1990, Review of Educational Research](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543060002265).
- Kulik 1990 overall d = 0.52; less able students d = 0.61; more able d = 0.40 (difference not significant); restricted to standardized (not teacher-made) tests the effect fell to d = 0.08. Slavin's (1987) best-evidence synthesis found effects nearer d = 0.25 once instructional time was controlled — [Nintil review reporting Kulik 1990 and Slavin 1987](https://nintil.com/bloom-sigma/).
- VanLehn (2011), Educational Psychologist 46(4): human tutoring d = 0.79 vs. no tutoring (not the believed 2.0); intelligent tutoring systems d = 0.76 — "nearly as effective as human tutoring"; prior beliefs of answer-based 0.3 / ITS 1.0 / human 2.0 were not confirmed — [ERIC record EJ946764 (abstract)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ946764); [Taylor & Francis record](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00461520.2011.611369).
- Secondary confirmation of VanLehn's granularity breakdown: step-based tutoring 0.76 ("almost as effective as human tutoring"), substep-based 0.40, human 0.79 — [Alkhatlan & Kalita ITS survey, arXiv 1812.09628, §5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1812.09628).
- Other ITS meta-analyses (secondary report in the same survey): Ma et al. 2014 (107 effect sizes, 73 studies) ITS vs teacher-led large-group d = 0.42, vs non-ITS CBI 0.57, vs textbooks 0.35, vs individual human tutoring −0.11 (n.s.), vs small group 0.05; Kulik & Fletcher (2015/16) 50 studies, median gain 0.66 on local tests but 0.13 on standardized tests; Steenbergen-Hu & Cooper 2013 (K-12 math) no significant short-term effect, larger when used a full year — [Alkhatlan & Kalita survey §5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1812.09628).
- DARPA's Digital Tutor (Navy IT training) reached d = 1.97–3.18, the one modern result that approaches or exceeds Bloom's figure — [Nintil review](https://nintil.com/bloom-sigma/) (secondary; primary is Fletcher & Morrison IDA reports).
- Feedback: Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie (2020), 435 studies / 994 effect sizes / 61,000+ participants: overall d = 0.48 (CI 0.44–0.51) under a random-effects model; 17% of effects negative; high-information feedback (task + process + self-regulation) d = 0.99; corrective feedback d = 0.46; reinforcement/punishment d = 0.24; cognitive outcomes d = 0.51, motivational 0.33; the earlier d = 0.79 (Hattie & Timperley 2007) was revised downward due to duplicate studies, fixed-effects modelling and looser inclusion — [Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie 2020, Frontiers in Psychology (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6987456/).
- Shute (2008) summarizing earlier meta-analyses: feedback generally improves learning "ranging from about .40 of a standard deviation (Guzzo, Jette, & Katzell, 1985) to .80 and higher (Azevedo & Bernard, 1995; Kluger & DeNisi, 1996)", with negative findings historically ignored (Kluger & DeNisi 1996) — [Shute 2007 ETS report RR-07-11 (the report version of Shute 2008 RER), §5](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/shute%202007_f.pdf).
- Retrieval practice: Rowland (2014) g = 0.50 (testing vs restudy), stronger with complex material, effortful retrieval and feedback; Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017), 217 studies, g = 0.61, classroom ≈ lab; the two disagree on format (Adesope: MC > short answer; Rowland: the reverse) — [Frontiers 2018 review citing both](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02412/full); [Adesope et al. 2017, RER](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.3102/0034654316689306).
- Self-explanation: Bisra, Liu, Nesbit, Salimi & Winne (2018), 69 effect sizes from 64 reports, random-effects g = 0.55; benefits found for most school subjects and for both conceptual and procedural knowledge — [Bisra et al. 2018, Educational Psychology Review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x); [ERIC EJ1186664](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1186664).
- Learning by teaching: Kobayashi (2019), 28 studies: preparing-to-teach g = 0.35; preparing-to-teach plus actually teaching g = 0.56, vs studying without teaching expectancy; effects held for deep and surface learning and after delay — [Kobayashi 2019, Japanese Psychological Research](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jpr.12221). Fiorella's summary: d ≈ 0.30–0.40 for preparing to teach vs preparing for a test; d ≈ 0.5–0.6 when students also teach — [Fiorella, "Learning by Teaching" (UNH summary)](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-learning-by-teaching-fiorella.pdf).
- Productive failure: Sinha & Kapur (2021), 53 studies / 166 comparisons / >12,000 participants: problem-solving-then-instruction (PS-I) beats instruction-then-problem-solving with g = 0.36 (CI 0.20–0.51) on conceptual understanding and transfer, no procedural cost; fidelity to PF design principles raises it to d ≈ 0.58; **effects reverse (favor I-PS) for grades 2–5 and for domain-general skills** — [Sinha & Kapur 2021, Review of Educational Research](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543211019105); [ERIC EJ1308129](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1308129).
- Expertise reversal (Tetzlaff et al. 2025, Learning and Instruction), 60 experiments / 5,924 participants: low-prior-knowledge learners learn better with high-assistance instruction d = 0.505; high-prior-knowledge learners learn better with low assistance d = −0.428; effect asymmetric (assisting novices matters more than withholding assistance from experts); moderated by prior-knowledge assessment type, education level, domain; weaker for younger students and for humanities/language — [Tetzlaff et al. 2025, Learning and Instruction](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959475225000660); [Tips for Teachers summary](https://tipsforteachers.co.uk/research-a-cornerstone-of-adaptivity-a-meta-analysis-of-the-expertise-reversal-effect/).
- Undergraduate STEM active learning: Freeman et al. (2014), 225 studies: exam/concept-inventory performance +0.47 SD under active learning (n = 158); odds of failing 1.95× under lecture (n = 67); lecture students 1.5× more likely to fail — [Freeman et al. 2014, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/abs/10.1073/pnas.1319030111).
- Socratic/dialogic tutoring: Chi et al. (2001), Cognitive Science 25(4): when tutors were suppressed from giving explanations and feedback and restricted to prompting, "students learned just as effectively"; learning attributed to deeper/more scaffolding episodes and students reading more — [ASU record of Chi et al. 2001](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/learning-from-human-tutoring/). AutoTutor (natural-language dialogue tutor using expectation-misconception tailoring) shows ~0.5–0.6 SD gains; over 100 hours of naturalistic tutoring showed human tutors rarely use "bona fide Socratic tutoring strategies, modeling-scaffolding-fading, reciprocal teaching, frontier learning, building on prerequisites, or diagnosis/remediation" — [Graesser et al., AutoTutor chapter (Memphis)](https://blogs.memphis.edu/aolney/files/2019/10/AutoTutor-chapter-olney_publications.pdf); [Graesser et al. 2001 IJAIED preprint](https://people.cs.pitt.edu/~litman/courses/cs3710_s02/graesser.pdf).
- Chi & Wylie (2014) ICAP: learning ordered Interactive > Constructive > Active > Passive — [Chi & Wylie 2014, Educational Psychologist (ASU PDF)](https://education.asu.edu/sites/g/files/litvpz656/files/lcl/chiwylie2014icap_2.pdf).
- Dunlosky et al. (2013): practice testing and distributed practice rated **high utility**; elaborative interrogation, self-explanation and interleaving **moderate**; ~400 studies reviewed — [Dunlosky et al. 2013, PSPI (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26173288/); [AFT summary by Dunlosky](https://www.aft.org/ae/fall2013/dunlosky).
- Hattie's Visible Learning methodology critiques: Bergeron (2017) and Simpson (2017) show the effect size for the same experiment "can differ enormously (0 to infinity!) depending on how it is calculated"; Hattie converts correlations into effect sizes; Simpson: "standardised effect size is a research tool for individual studies, not a policy tool for directing whole educational areas"; Bergeron: many included studies are short interventions with volunteer learners, researchers delivering the teaching — [Structural Learning review of the critiques](https://www.structural-learning.com/post/visible-learning-a-teachers-guide); [Gelman blog on Hattie](https://statmodeling.stat.columbia.edu/2018/09/01/john-hatties-visible-learning-much-trust-influential-review-education-research/); [Nordic Psychology 2021 critique](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/19012276.2021.1962731). Hattie's own 2020 feedback re-analysis (above) halving the 2007 estimate is the concrete instance.

### Inferences
- The right prior for any single well-implemented technique in an AI tutor is d ≈ 0.3–0.6 on proximal (course-aligned) measures and much less on distal standardized measures (Kulik 1990: 0.52 → 0.08; Kulik & Fletcher: 0.66 → 0.13). Do not design or promise around 2 SD.
- The "hold the student to a mastery criterion" mechanism that inflated Bloom's number is itself the actionable lesson: the loop should not advance a student who has not demonstrated mastery, rather than trying to reproduce 2σ by cleverness.
- VanLehn's finding that step-based ITS ≈ human tutors while substep (finer) systems do worse suggests interaction granularity below "one solution step" adds cost without gain; an LLM tutor should scaffold at the step level, not micromanage every token of reasoning. **[extrapolation]**
- Chi 2001 + Graesser: the learning comes from the student constructing, not from the tutor's explanation quality — an LLM's fluency advantage is therefore not where the leverage is. **[extrapolation]**

### Gaps
- Could not fetch VanLehn's answer-based effect size (widely quoted as d ≈ 0.31) from a primary or reliable secondary source; only step (0.76), substep (0.40) and human (0.79) were confirmed.
- Cohen, Kulik & Kulik 1982 (tutoring d ≈ 0.40) and Nickow, Oreopoulos & Quan 2020 (tutoring d ≈ 0.37) were not retrieved; the Nintil review mentions the tutoring literature but I did not verify those numbers.
- No meta-analytic effect size specifically for "Socratic questioning" as an isolated treatment was found; the evidence is indirect (Chi 2001 prompting-only tutors; AutoTutor; ICAP).

## KQ2. How should the tutor probe a student's current knowledge efficiently — how many questions, how to pick difficulty (CAT logic), when to stop, how to tell a slip from a misconception, how to handle a total novice?

### Takeaway
CAT logic: pick each item to maximize Fisher information at the current ability estimate (Rasch: items the student has ~50% chance on), stop when SE(θ) falls below ~0.3 logits or a min/max item cap hits (PROMIS: 4–12 items, SE < 0.32); in practice well-targeted CATs converge in 4–6 items. Fixed-SE rules fail for extreme (very low) ability, so cap the probe and treat "everything wrong" as a floor, not as a precise estimate. Slip vs misconception is distinguished by consistency across isomorphic items, by reasoning tiers, and by confidence — not by a single wrong answer.

### Cited Findings
- Maximum-information item selection: "The largest information, and so the smallest standard error, occurs when P=0.5" (item difficulty matched to person ability); SE = 1/sqrt(Σ P(1−P)); precision improves slowly — SE 0.15 needs ~10× the items of SE 0.5; targeting easier items costs length: P=0.6 +5%, P=0.7 +20%, P=0.8 +60%, P=0.9 nearly triples test length; for a 95%-confidence pass/fail decision the count ranges from ~16 items when the person is 1 logit from the cut to >4,000 at 0.1 logit — [Linacre, Rasch Measurement Transactions 20:2 "CAT item selection, standard errors and stopping rules"](https://www.rasch.org/rmt/rmt202f.htm).
- Three standard stopping rules: fixed length; standard-error threshold; minimum-information (stop when no remaining item can add a preset amount of information). Choi, Grady & Dodd (2011) use SE = 0.3 as the criterion; simulated CATs averaged 4–6 items (30-item pool) and 3.8–5.8 items (90-item pool); at θ = −2 a fixed-SE rule "would administer all 28 items in the pool, even though the desired standard error is unobtainable" because information is scarce at extreme trait levels — [Choi, Grady & Dodd 2011, Educational and Psychological Measurement (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3028267/).
- PROMIS default CAT stopping rule: minimum 4 / maximum 12 items, or stop when SE(θ) < 0.32; predicted-standard-error-reduction (PSER) rules administer fewer items when predicted gains are small — [Reducing patient burden of PROMs through advanced CAT stopping rules (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12681468/).
- Stopping rules for banks with non-uniform information: a fixed SE threshold is unreachable where the bank has few informative items, motivating minimum-information/change-in-θ rules — [Stopping Rules for CAT When Item Banks Have Nonuniform Information (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7518406/).
- Knowledge Space Theory (Doignon & Falmagne 1985) assessment logic: a "surmise" relation encodes prerequisites; the *outer fringe* of a student's knowledge state is "the set of items the student is 'ready to learn'" and the fringe items have maximal diagnostic value in adaptive assessment — [ALEKS: Knowledge Space Theory](https://www.aleks.com/about_aleks/knowledge_space_theory); [Practical perspective on KST: ALEKS and its data, J. Math. Psych. 2021](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022249621000134).
- Bayesian Knowledge Tracing models a "slip" as P(wrong | mastered) and a "guess" as P(right | not mastered); models are theoretically degenerate when guess or slip > 0.5; parameters suffer identifiability (same data fit by different parameter sets) — [Van de Sande, "Properties of the BKT model" (ERIC)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1115329.pdf); [Baker, Corbett & Aleven 2008, contextual slip/guess estimation](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/BCA2008W.pdf).
- BKT's slip probability is a performance statistic, not a diagnosis: "A probability of success is not the same thing as a diagnosis of misconception" — [eduKate explainer on knowledge tracing](https://edukatesg.com/2026/09/11/how-knowledge-tracing-works-estimate-what-a-learner-knows-from-the-pattern-of-their-attempts/) (secondary, non-academic).
- FCI design for separating noise from misconception: false negatives (a Newtonian thinker picks a non-Newtonian option) judged "certainly less than ten percent (fewer than three questions 'missed')" and attributable to "carelessness or inattention"; false positives cannot be eliminated ("even random choices have a 20% chance"); two mitigations — probe each conceptual dimension with several questions in different contexts so one false positive is offset, and use distracters "culled from extensive student interviews"; the FCI "should be administered and interpreted as a whole; separate pieces of it are much less reliable and informative" — [Hestenes & Halloun 1995, The Physics Teacher 33:502](https://davidhestenes.net/modeling/R&E/InterFCI.pdf).
- FCI thresholds: 60% = "entry threshold" to Newtonian thinking; 85% = "mastery threshold" (Hestenes & Halloun 1995) — [FCI chapter, UMD PERG](https://physics.umd.edu/perg/dissertations/Saul/Chapter4.PDF); [Wikipedia: Force Concept Inventory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force_Concept_Inventory).
- Two-tier tests (answer + reason) do not separate misconception from lack of knowledge; three-tier tests add a confidence rating for that purpose (Hasan, Bagayoko & Kelley 1999 proposed certainty of response to distinguish "misconceptions and lack of knowledge"); four-tier adds confidence on both tiers — [Review of four-tier diagnostic tests, J. Turkish Science Education](https://www.tused.org/index.php/tused/article/download/1180/819/7915).
- Wood, Wood & Middleton (1978) "contingent shift principle": if the learner fails, increase the level of support on the next move; if they succeed, decrease it; contingent instruction outperformed three less-responsive regimes — [Johnson, "A focus on scaffolding" (secondary summary)](https://johnsonphoenix.substack.com/p/a-focus-on-scaffolding); [Van de Pol, Volman & Beishuizen 2010, Educational Psychology Review, scaffolding decade review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-010-9127-6).

### Inferences
- Concrete probe rule: start at the middle of the expected distribution (or at the outer fringe of the last known state), choose the next item at ~50–70% expected success (the 0.7 target costs only ~20% more items and feels better to the student), and stop after ~4–6 items per skill when the estimate has stabilized or after a hard cap of ~8–12; SE 0.3 on a unit-variance ability scale corresponds to reliability ≈ 0.91 (1 − SE²) **[inference from the Rasch SE formula]**.
- For a total novice ("everything wrong"): the CAT literature says precision is unobtainable at θ ≈ −2 and the fixed-SE rule just burns items; the correct behavior is to stop early on a floor hit (e.g. 3 consecutive misses at the easiest available level), classify as "below the item bank," and switch from probing to teaching the lowest prerequisite (KST: work from the inner fringe). **[inference from Choi 2011 + KST]**
- Slip vs misconception decision rule that follows from Hestenes & Halloun and the tiered-test literature: one wrong answer = unknown; wrong on ≥2 isomorphic items in different surface contexts with the *same* distractor family = misconception; wrong once then right on the re-ask = slip; correct answer + wrong reasoning (tier-2) = false positive, treat as not-known; low confidence + wrong = lack of knowledge (teach), high confidence + wrong = misconception (confront). The 10% false-negative ceiling implies ~1 in 10 wrong answers by a competent student is noise. **[inference]**
- BKT-style priors (slip ≈ 0.1) are consistent with the FCI's <10% false-negative estimate, so a single miss should move mastery belief only modestly. **[inference]**

### Gaps
- No source retrieved for typical fitted BKT slip/guess values in Cognitive Tutor data (commonly quoted ~0.1 slip / ~0.2–0.3 guess); treat those as unverified.
- No study found that specifies the minimum item count for a *learning* (as opposed to measurement) probe — CAT numbers come from psychometric and health-outcome contexts and are extrapolated here.
- ALEKS's actual initial-assessment length (often quoted as 20–30 questions) was not confirmed from an ALEKS/KST source in this session.

## KQ3. How should the tutor choose what to teach next — edge of ZPD, prerequisite order, and the "85% rule"?

### Takeaway
Teach at the outer fringe of the current knowledge state (KST) — the ZPD operationalized — using a difficulty target around 80–85% success, which is what Wilson et al. 2019 derive for gradient-descent binary classifiers and what perceptual-learning staircases already use; but the 85% number is a **model result**, explicitly not yet generalized to multi-choice tasks, non-gradient learners, or classroom education.

### Cited Findings
- Wood, Bruner & Ross (1976) define scaffolding as enabling "a child or novice to solve a problem, carry out a task or achieve a goal which would be beyond his unassisted efforts" and list six functions: recruitment, reduction of degrees of freedom, direction maintenance, marking critical features, frustration control, demonstration — [Johnson, "A focus on scaffolding" (secondary)](https://johnsonphoenix.substack.com/p/a-focus-on-scaffolding); [Van de Pol et al. 2010 decade review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-010-9127-6).
- Wilson, Shenhav, Straccia & Cohen (2019), Nature Communications 10:4646: for binary classification with stochastic-gradient-descent learning rules and Gaussian noise, the optimal training error rate is 15.87% (training accuracy ≈ 85%); optimal-accuracy training proceeds "exponentially faster than training at a fixed difficulty"; demonstrated on a perceptron, a two-layer network, and the Law & Gold model of perceptual learning — [Wilson et al. 2019, Nature Communications](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-12552-4); text read from the [eScholarship open-access copy](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/0v22s4g4).
- Wilson et al.'s own caveats (Discussion): the optimum shifts with noise distribution (85% Gaussian, 82% Laplacian, 75% Cauchy); "it remains to be generalized to a broader class of circumstances, such as multi-choice tasks and different learning algorithms"; a Bayesian learner with perfect memory has no difficulty sweet spot at all (order of examples is irrelevant to it); "no formal theoretical work has addressed the effect of training accuracy on learning, a test of which is an important direction for future work"; they position the result as "the first step towards a more mathematical instantiation" of Region of Proximal Learning / desirable difficulties / Vygotsky, and note that psychophysics staircases are designed to hold accuracy at ~80–85% — [Wilson et al. 2019 (eScholarship PDF)](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/0v22s4g4).
- KST: the outer fringe is the set of items that can be validly added to the current state ("ready to learn") — [ALEKS KST page](https://www.aleks.com/about_aleks/knowledge_space_theory).
- Productive failure boundary conditions: PS-I wins for conceptual understanding/transfer (g = 0.36) but the sign flips for grades 2–5 and for domain-general skills — [Sinha & Kapur 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543211019105).
- Expertise reversal: assistance helps low-prior-knowledge learners (d = 0.505) and hurts high-prior-knowledge learners (d = −0.428); "Regular assessment is necessary to optimally adjust instruction over time"; when uncertain, provide support — [Tetzlaff et al. 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959475225000660); [summary](https://tipsforteachers.co.uk/research-a-cornerstone-of-adaptivity-a-meta-analysis-of-the-expertise-reversal-effect/).

### Inferences
- Operational ZPD = KST outer fringe: pick the next concept whose prerequisites are all in the mastered state and which the student has not yet demonstrated. Prerequisite order is not optional — Graesser's naturalistic-tutoring analysis lists "building on prerequisites" among the strategies human tutors *fail* to use, and it is one an AI can do mechanically. **[extrapolation]**
- Use the 85% rule as a *control target for practice-item difficulty* (keep running accuracy within ~75–90%; if the student is above 90%, step up; below ~70%, step down or scaffold) — not as a claim about human learning rates. Its status is [theory/model]; the human-side support is the older desirable-difficulties and staircase practice, not a classroom effect size. **[extrapolation]**
- The 85% rule and productive failure are not in conflict if read carefully: PF is a *sequence* decision (struggle before instruction on a new concept) while 85% is a *steady-state practice* target after instruction. **[inference]**

### Gaps
- Vygotsky 1978 primary text was not retrieved; ZPD is cited here through secondary scaffolding literature only.
- No empirical study found that tests an ~85% success target against other targets on human academic learning outcomes.

## KQ4. What should a single teaching step look like — worked example vs problem first, when to scaffold and when to fade?

### Takeaway
For a novice on a new concept: high assistance (worked example / direct explanation) wins (d ≈ 0.5); as prior knowledge rises, fade to problem solving because assistance starts to hurt (d ≈ −0.43). For older learners and conceptual/transfer goals, a bounded struggle attempt *before* instruction adds ~0.36 SD. Fade via contingency: raise support after failure, lower after success.

### Cited Findings
- Expertise reversal effect: instructional assistance "leads to increased learning gains in novices, but decreased learning gains in experts" (an aptitude–treatment interaction); as learners gain expertise "worked examples lose their effectiveness", and faded worked examples structure the transition to problem solving — [Wikipedia: Expertise reversal effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expertise_reversal_effect); [Wikipedia: Worked-example effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worked-example_effect) (secondary; primary Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller 2003; Sweller & Cooper 1985).
- Meta-analytic magnitudes and asymmetry (assisting novices matters more than withholding assistance from experts); effects strongest in higher education, weaker in primary and in humanities/language — [Tetzlaff et al. 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959475225000660).
- PS-I vs I-PS: g = 0.36 for conceptual/transfer, up to 0.58 with high design fidelity; reversal for young children and domain-general skills — [Sinha & Kapur 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543211019105). Explicit failure-driven scaffolding (guiding the exploration toward failure) replicates robustly — [Learning and Instruction 2021 replication](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959475221000475).
- Contingent shift: increase support after failure, decrease after success; six scaffolding functions incl. reduction of degrees of freedom and marking critical features — [Johnson summary of Wood et al. 1976 / Wood, Wood & Middleton 1978](https://johnsonphoenix.substack.com/p/a-focus-on-scaffolding).
- Shute guideline 3: "Present elaborated feedback in manageable units ... A stepwise presentation of feedback ... gives learners sufficient information to correct errors on their own"; guideline 9: "Provide feedback after learners have attempted a solution. Do not let learners see answers before trying"; guideline 16: "Avoid using progressive hints that always terminate with the correct answer" — [Shute 2007/2008 Tables 2–3](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/shute%202007_f.pdf).
- Shute guideline 28: "For low-achieving learners, use scaffolding"; 26: novices need "explicit, directive feedback", hints "may not be as helpful"; 27/29: high-achievers benefit from "hints, cues, and prompts" and verification-only feedback may suffice — [Shute Table 5](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/shute%202007_f.pdf).
- Step-based ITS (feedback and hints on each solution step) matched human tutors (0.76 vs 0.79); substep systems did not (0.40) — [VanLehn 2011 via Alkhatlan & Kalita](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1812.09628).
- Kestin et al. (2025), Scientific Reports, RCT n = 194 Harvard intro-physics students, crossover over two weeks: AI tutor (built on the same research-based pedagogy as the active-learning class) produced median post-test 4.5 vs 3.5, "learning gains more than double", effect size reported in the 0.73–1.3 SD range, in less time, with higher engagement — [Kestin et al. 2025, Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-97652-6); numbers read from [ETC Journal review](https://etcjournal.com/2025/11/10/review-of-kestin-et-al-s-june-2025-harvard-study-on-ai-tutoring/) (secondary).
- Bastani et al. (2025), PNAS, ~1,000 Turkish high-school math students: unrestricted GPT-4 ("GPT Base") raised practice performance 48% but lowered unassisted exam performance 17% vs control; "GPT Tutor" (teacher-designed hints, no answer give-away) raised practice performance 127% and left exam performance ≈ control (≈ −0.01 SD vs −0.19 SD for Base) — [Bastani et al. 2025, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2422633122); numbers read from [Knowledge at Wharton summary](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/without-guardrails-generative-ai-can-harm-education/) and the search-surfaced SD figures (secondary).

### Inferences
- A teaching step for a **novice** (concept not yet in state): (1) one bounded attempt at a target problem *without* the answer visible (PF + Shute #9), 1–3 minutes, if the student is ≥ secondary age and the goal is conceptual; (2) then a worked example or direct explanation in small chunks (Shute #3, worked-example effect); (3) then a near-transfer problem with step-level hints (VanLehn step granularity). For a **developing** student: skip (2), go problem-first with hints on request. For a **proficient** student: problem only, verification feedback (Shute #29). **[inference synthesizing the above]**
- Fading is a per-concept state machine driven by the last outcome (contingent shift), not by elapsed time. **[inference]**
- The Kestin/Bastani contrast is the strongest LLM-specific evidence: the same class of model produces +1 SD or −0.2 SD depending on whether the prompt enforces "one step at a time, no answer give-away, check understanding." Guardrails are not optional. **[classroom/RCT-proven for the two specific designs; extrapolation to other domains]**

### Gaps
- Sweller & Cooper 1985 / Kalyuga 2003 primaries not retrieved; the worked-example effect size in isolation (often quoted d ≈ 0.5–0.6 for novices) is not independently verified here beyond the Tetzlaff meta-analysis's "high assistance" category.
- Kestin 2025's exact prompt principles and stated limitations were truncated in the secondary source; the primary is paywalled behind a Nature IDP redirect from this environment.

## KQ5. How should the tutor check understanding — MC vs free response vs self-explanation vs teach-back, and what does each measure?

### Takeaway
Multiple choice with research-derived distractors is a valid, cheap *recognition* probe (Bloom: remember/understand) only when several items per concept are aggregated; add a reasoning tier to catch right-answer-wrong-reason (false positives) and a confidence tier to separate misconception from ignorance. Free response, self-explanation (g = 0.55) and teach-back (g = 0.35–0.56) are both assessments *and* interventions and reach apply/analyze/evaluate; retrieval practice of any format (g ≈ 0.5–0.6) beats restudy.

### Cited Findings
- "The validity of a concept test arises primarily from its distractors"; distractors "represent scientifically problematic ideas that students actually hold, presented in students' own recognizable language"; development goes from open-ended responses and think-aloud interviews to distractors — [AAAS "Concept Inventories: Tools for Uncovering STEM Students' Misconceptions" (Richardson)](https://www.aaas.org/sites/default/files/02_AER_Richardson.pdf); [Wikipedia: Concept inventory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concept_inventory).
- FCI: "with one exception, to every question ... there is precisely one and only one Newtonian response among the five alternatives"; a taxonomy of non-Newtonian responses (Table II of Hestenes, Wells & Swackhamer 1992) maps distractors to misconceptions; individual items are unreliable in isolation — [Hestenes & Halloun 1995](https://davidhestenes.net/modeling/R&E/InterFCI.pdf).
- Limitations: "Some wrong answers given by students were not included in the answer options of the original FCI, and pupils who answered multiple-choice questions correctly were not necessarily able to give correct justification" — [NTNU FCI evaluation, arXiv 1504.06099](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1504.06099).
- Two-tier tests: tier 1 MC content, tier 2 the reason (open or MC); "The correct answers to multiple choice items do not guarantee the right reason being present"; Hestenes & Halloun recommended tracking correct-answer/wrong-reason (false positive) and wrong-answer/right-reason (false negative) as validity evidence — [ERIC EJ1456849 two-tier test development](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1456849.pdf); [J. Turkish Science Education four-tier review](https://www.tused.org/index.php/tused/article/download/1180/819/7915).
- Three-/four-tier tests add confidence to separate misconception from lack of knowledge, which two-tier tests cannot do — [four-tier review](https://www.tused.org/index.php/tused/article/download/1180/819/7915).
- Retrieval practice: g = 0.50 (Rowland 2014) / 0.61 (Adesope 2017); format moderator disputed (Adesope MC > SA; Rowland SA > MC) — [Frontiers 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02412/full).
- Self-explanation prompts: g = 0.55 across 64 reports, effective for conceptual and procedural knowledge — [Bisra et al. 2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x).
- Teach-back: preparing-to-teach g = 0.35, teaching g = 0.56 (Kobayashi 2019); benefits persist after delay and cover deep learning — [Kobayashi 2019](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jpr.12221).
- Generating teaching materials for others: a separate meta-analysis exists (Kobayashi 2022, Educational Research Review) — [ScienceDirect record](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1747938X22000446). Effect size not read.
- ICAP: interactive > constructive > active > passive; MC recognition is "active" at best, explanation and teaching are "constructive"/"interactive" — [Chi & Wylie 2014](https://education.asu.edu/sites/g/files/litvpz656/files/lcl/chiwylie2014icap_2.pdf).
- Metacognitive check: students in Deslauriers et al. (2019) active-learning sections scored 0.46 SD *higher* on the test of learning yet rated their own learning 0.56 SD *lower* than passive-lecture peers (n = 149 Harvard physics students, randomized crossover); explanation: fluent lecturing is misread as learning, novices lack metacognitive calibration, effort is misread as failure — [Deslauriers et al. 2019, PNAS (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6765278/).
- Foresight bias (Koriat & Bjork 2005): judgments of learning are inflated whenever information present at study but absent at test (the target) makes the cue seem more informative than it will be; remedies: delayed JOLs, test experience, and judging with the cue alone — [Koriat & Bjork 2005, JEP:LMC (Bjork Lab PDF)](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Koriat_RBjork_2005.pdf); follow-up: illusions "can be remedied by manipulations that enhance learners' sensitivity to retrieval conditions at test" — [Koriat & Bjork 2006, Memory & Cognition](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03193244).

### Inferences
- Mapping to Bloom's taxonomy (**[inference]**, no source maps these formats to Bloom levels empirically): distractor-MC → remember/understand (and *which* misconception, if the distractor is diagnostic); short free response with a numeric/structural answer → apply; "explain why" / self-explanation → understand/analyze; teach-back ("explain it to a classmate who thinks X") → analyze/evaluate and exposes gaps the student cannot feel (counters foresight bias, since the target is absent).
- Never treat a student's "I get it" as evidence — Deslauriers and Koriat & Bjork both show self-report is anti-correlated or inflated. Check with a cue-only retrieval task, delayed at least past the immediate turn. **[inference from lab and classroom evidence]**
- Use MC for the fast probe (KQ2), then require a one-sentence reason (tier 2) before crediting mastery; ask for confidence on the wrong ones to route to "teach" (low confidence) vs "confront" (high confidence). **[inference]**
- LLM-graded free response is where an AI tutor is *cheaper* than ITS history (which had to hand-author step models); but the FCI experience says distractors must come from real student reasoning, not from the model's imagination — the tutor should harvest actual wrong reasons over time. **[extrapolation]**

### Gaps
- No quantitative false-positive rate (share of correct MC answers with wrong reasoning) was extracted from the tiered-test literature; the four-tier review PDF's numeric sections did not parse.
- Chi, de Leeuw, Chiu & LaVancher 1994 (original self-explanation training study) not retrieved; only the 2018 meta-analysis.
- Fiorella & Mayer 2013 primary not retrieved; numbers come from Kobayashi 2019 and Fiorella's own summary.

## KQ6. How should the tutor respond to a wrong answer — feedback timing and type?

### Takeaway
Feedback content matters far more than valence or timing: high-information (task + process + self-regulation) d ≈ 0.99 vs praise/reinforcement 0.24; praise and normative comparison can be negative. Timing evidence is inconsistent overall, but the consistent moderator is learner level and task difficulty: immediate, directive, elaborated feedback for novices and hard tasks; delayed, facilitative (hint/prompt) feedback for proficient students, simple tasks, and transfer goals. Feedback must come after an attempt, in small units, and never via hint chains that end in the answer.

### Cited Findings
- Hattie & Timperley (2007) four levels: task (what is correct/incorrect and how to fix), process (strategies/relations among components), self-regulation (monitoring one's own learning), self (praise, person-directed); task/process/self-regulation are beneficial, self-level is ineffective "because it does not give the student any information in order to attain learning goals" — [BERA blog summarizing Hattie & Timperley 2007](https://www.bera.ac.uk/blog/how-to-optimise-the-use-of-hattie-and-timperleys-feedback-levels-for-student-learning) (secondary); primary: [Hattie & Timperley 2007, RER](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/003465430298487).
- Wisniewski et al. 2020: high-information feedback d = 0.99; corrective d = 0.46; reinforcement/punishment d = 0.24; motivational outcomes hurt when feedback is "controlling, negative and uninformative"; immediate vs delayed: "inconsistent results" — [Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie 2020 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6987456/).
- Shute 2008 "things to do": (1) focus on the task, not the learner; (2) elaborated feedback describing "the what, how, and/or why" beats verification; (3) manageable units, stepwise; (4) specific and clear; (5) "as simple as possible but no simpler" — Kulhavy et al. 1985 found over-complex feedback did not help; (6) reduce uncertainty between performance and goals; (7) unbiased, written/computer feedback is taken more seriously than human oral feedback (Kluger & DeNisi); (8) promote a learning-goal orientation, frame mistakes as part of learning; (9) only after an attempt — [Shute 2007/2008, Table 2](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/shute%202007_f.pdf).
- "Things to avoid": (10) no normative comparisons; (11) comments-only beat grades and grades+comments (Wiliam: grades-only no gain, comments-only large gain, grades+comments no gain); (12) nothing that threatens self-esteem or is controlling; (13) "Use praise sparingly, if at all"; (15) do not interrupt a learner actively engaged in solving; (16) avoid progressive hints that always terminate in the correct answer (Aleven & Koedinger 2000 hint abuse); (18) "Minimize use of extensive error analyses and diagnosis" — cost may exceed benefit and analyses are rarely complete (Sleeman et al. 1989; VanLehn et al. 2005) — [Shute Table 3](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/shute%202007_f.pdf).
- Timing: (19) immediate feedback "producing greater immediate gains and more efficient learning" (Corbett & Anderson 2001) but delayed feedback "associated with better transfer" (Schroth 1992); (20) difficult tasks → immediate; (21) simple tasks → delayed (avoid intrusion); (22) retention of procedural/conceptual knowledge → immediate (wide support); (23) transfer → consider delayed, "needs more research" — [Shute Table 4](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/shute%202007_f.pdf).
- Learner characteristics: (24) high achievers → delayed; (25) low achievers → immediate; (26) low achievers → directive/corrective, hints "may not be as helpful"; (27) high achievers → facilitative (hints, cues, prompts); (28) low achievers → scaffolding; (29) high achievers → verification may suffice; (30) low achievers → correct response plus elaboration; Mason & Bruning (2001): immediate for low achievers on simple or complex tasks, delayed for high achievers especially on complex tasks — [Shute Table 5 and §4.4](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/shute%202007_f.pdf).
- Feedback effect sizes are heterogeneous and can be negative: 17% of 994 effects negative (Wisniewski 2020); Kluger & DeNisi's 1996 finding of frequent negative effects is cited by Shute — [Wisniewski 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6987456/); [Shute §5](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/shute%202007_f.pdf).
- Retrieval practice benefits are larger "when feedback was given during practice" (Rowland 2014) — [Frontiers 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02412/full).
- Misconception-tailored dialogue: AutoTutor's expectation-misconception tailoring (compare the student's answer against expected good answers and anticipated misconceptions, then pump/hint/prompt/assert) is "frequent in human tutoring" and "helps students learn" — [Graesser AutoTutor chapter](https://blogs.memphis.edu/aolney/files/2019/10/AutoTutor-chapter-olney_publications.pdf).
- Bastani 2025: answer-giving AI raised practice scores and lowered unassisted exam scores; hint-only AI did not harm — [Wharton summary of Bastani et al. PNAS 2025](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/without-guardrails-generative-ai-can-harm-education/).

### Inferences
- A wrong-answer policy that follows directly from the evidence: (a) if the student is a novice on this concept or the item is hard for them → immediate, task-level, corrective + short "why" (Shute #20, #22, #25, #26, #30); (b) if proficient → delay the verdict, give a process-level prompt or cue and let them retry (Shute #24, #27, #29); (c) never praise the person, never compare to others, never grade mid-loop (Shute #10–13); (d) hint ladders must not bottom out in the answer (Shute #16, Bastani); (e) do not diagnose exhaustively — a light EMT-style match of the student's answer against a small set of expected misconceptions is the evidence-backed level of diagnosis (Shute #18, AutoTutor).
- The Hattie & Timperley "self-regulation" level is the one an LLM tutor can supply cheaply and consistently ("what did you check before answering? what would tell you this is wrong?"), and Wisniewski's d = 0.99 for combined task+process+self-regulation content is the strongest single effect in this brief. **[extrapolation from meta-analytic moderator]**
- Shute #7 (computer-delivered feedback perceived as less biased) is a genuine structural advantage for an AI tutor; #14 (avoid oral delivery) argues against voice-first correction. **[inference]**

### Gaps
- Shute's tables cite primary timing studies (Clariana 1990; Schroth 1992; Kulhavy 1985) that were not read directly; her review labels the transfer/delay finding "needs more research".
- Kluger & DeNisi 1996 (d = 0.41, ~38% of effects negative) is widely quoted but was not verified from the primary in this session.

## KQ7. Which of these have strong effect sizes vs weak/contested ones, and what is proven in classrooms vs plausible extrapolation to an LLM tutor?

### Takeaway
Strong and replicated: retrieval practice, feedback with high information content, step-level tutoring/ITS, active learning in college STEM, self-explanation, expertise-reversal-driven fading. Moderate with boundary conditions: mastery learning (large local, tiny on standardized tests), learning-by-teaching, productive failure (reverses for young children). Contested or model-only: Bloom's 2σ, Hattie's aggregate rankings, the 85% rule as a human learning law, "Socratic questioning" as a distinct treatment, and any LLM-tutor claim beyond the two 2025 RCTs.

### Cited Findings (ranked)
- **Strong, classroom-replicated**: retrieval practice g = 0.50–0.61 across 217 studies incl. classrooms — [Adesope 2017](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.3102/0034654316689306); active learning in undergraduate STEM +0.47 SD, failure odds 1.95× under lecture, 225 studies — [Freeman 2014](https://www.pnas.org/doi/abs/10.1073/pnas.1319030111); high-information feedback d = 0.99 (overall feedback 0.48) — [Wisniewski 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6987456/); step-based ITS 0.76 ≈ human 0.79 — [VanLehn 2011](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ946764); self-explanation g = 0.55 — [Bisra 2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x); expertise reversal d = +0.505 / −0.428 across 60 experiments — [Tetzlaff 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959475225000660).
- **Moderate / conditional**: mastery learning d = 0.52 (0.08 on standardized tests; Slavin ~0.25) — [Nintil on Kulik 1990 & Slavin 1987](https://nintil.com/bloom-sigma/); learning by teaching g = 0.35–0.56 — [Kobayashi 2019](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jpr.12221); productive failure g = 0.36 with age reversal — [Sinha & Kapur 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543211019105); ITS on standardized tests 0.13 vs 0.66 local — [Kulik & Fletcher via survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1812.09628).
- **Contested / overstated**: Bloom 2σ (mastery-criterion confound, never replicated at scale) — [Nintil](https://nintil.com/bloom-sigma/), [Education Next](https://www.educationnext.org/two-sigma-tutoring-separating-science-fiction-from-science-fact/); Hattie's Visible Learning aggregate effect sizes (correlation-to-d conversions, "0 to infinity" computational sensitivity, unrepresentative study contexts) — [Structural Learning summary of Bergeron 2017 & Simpson 2017](https://www.structural-learning.com/post/visible-learning-a-teachers-guide), [Gelman](https://statmodeling.stat.columbia.edu/2018/09/01/john-hatties-visible-learning-much-trust-influential-review-education-research/); feedback timing (inconsistent) — [Wisniewski 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6987456/).
- **Model-only**: the 85% rule (binary classification + SGD + Gaussian noise; authors say human test "is an important direction for future work") — [Wilson et al. 2019](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/0v22s4g4).
- **LLM-tutor specific (n = 2 RCTs)**: Kestin 2025 (+0.73–1.3 SD, n = 194, college physics, pedagogically constrained prompt) — [Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-97652-6); Bastani 2025 (−17% unassisted exam with unrestricted GPT-4; ≈0 with hint-only tutor; n ≈ 1,000 high-school math) — [PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2422633122).
- **College STEM specific**: Freeman 2014 (above); Deslauriers 2019 physics crossover (learning +0.46 SD, feeling-of-learning −0.56 SD) — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6765278/); Kestin 2025 physics (above); FCI thresholds 60%/85% for intro mechanics — [Hestenes & Halloun 1995](https://davidhestenes.net/modeling/R&E/InterFCI.pdf); expertise reversal effects "strongest in higher education" — [Tetzlaff 2025 summary](https://tipsforteachers.co.uk/research-a-cornerstone-of-adaptivity-a-meta-analysis-of-the-expertise-reversal-effect/).

### Inferences
- What transfers to an LLM tutor with the least extrapolation: retrieval practice with feedback, step-level hints, worked-example-then-fade for novices, self-explanation prompts, high-information task/process/self-regulation feedback, no answer give-away. These are format-agnostic behaviors that the ITS and classroom literature already tested; the LLM only changes the cost of authoring them. **[extrapolation, low risk]**
- What is plausible but unproven for an LLM tutor: CAT-style adaptive probing with LLM-generated items (item parameters are unknown, so "maximum information" is a heuristic, not a psychometric guarantee); LLM-invented distractors as misconception diagnostics (FCI's validity came from interview-harvested distractors); the 85% target. **[extrapolation, moderate risk]**
- What the evidence warns against: fluent, complete explanations on demand (Deslauriers fluency illusion + Bastani dependency), praise, grading mid-loop, and promising Bloom-scale gains.

### Gaps
- Only two LLM-tutor RCTs were located; no meta-analysis of LLM tutors exists yet in the sources found, and neither RCT measured retention beyond weeks.
- Hattie's specific Visible Learning numbers for "intelligent tutoring systems" and "mastery learning" were not retrieved and should not be quoted without checking the current edition.
