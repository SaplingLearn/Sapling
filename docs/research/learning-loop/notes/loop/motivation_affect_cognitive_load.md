# Motivation, Affect, and Cognitive Load in an AI Tutoring Loop for College Students

Scope note: covers SDT, flow/difficulty calibration, affect detection/response in ITS, gamification and streaks, growth mindset, goal setting/implementation intentions, cognitive load + Mayer's principles, learning styles (meshing hypothesis), peer/collaborative learning vs 1:1 AI, and over-reliance / metacognitive laziness. Evidence base is mixed K-12/college; each finding flags population where known. Several primary abstracts were paywalled or bot-blocked during this session (Baker et al. 2010 IJHCS; Patall et al. 2008; Deci et al. 1999; Kestin et al. 2025 full text) — where only secondary summaries were reachable this is stated and the exact number is placed in Gaps rather than asserted.

## KQ1. Productive difficulty band: detecting and responding to confusion, frustration, boredom from behavioral signals (response time, hedging, repeated errors, quitting, gaming)

### Takeaway
The validated ITS affect model is a state machine: engaged/flow → (impasse) → confusion → resolved (back to flow, with learning) or unresolved → frustration → boredom. Boredom is the state to fear (most persistent, linked to gaming the system and worst learning); confusion is beneficial ONLY when it is induced by a real impasse and then resolved; frustration is tolerable short-term ("better to be frustrated than bored"). Log-based detectors use fast actions after errors, help abuse, and systematic guessing; an affect-sensitive tutor that responds to these produced ~0.71σ gains for low-knowledge learners, but only once difficulty had built up (second session). A machine-learning result says the optimal training error rate is ~15% (85% accuracy) for gradient-descent-like learners — a reasonable engineering target for the difficulty band, not a human-validated constant.

### Cited Findings
- D'Mello & Graesser (2012, *Learning and Instruction* 22(2):145–157) model: learners in engagement/flow experience cognitive disequilibrium and confusion when they hit contradictions, incongruities, anomalies, obstacles to goals, and other impasses; they return to flow if equilibrium is restored through thought/reflection/problem solving; failure to restore equilibrium and blocked goals trigger frustration, which if unresolved leads to boredom. Confusion may benefit learning because learners are motivated to resolve it, leading to deeper understanding. — [ERIC EJ950444](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ950444); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0959475211000806)
- D'Mello, Lehman, Pekrun & Graesser (2014, *Learning and Instruction*), "Confusion can be beneficial for learning" — title/authorship confirmed via Semantic Scholar; abstract elided by publisher in this session. — [Semantic Scholar record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1016/j.learninstruc.2012.05.003?fields=title,abstract,year,authors); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/234102071_Confusion_can_be_beneficial_for_learning)
- Lehman, D'Mello, Strain, Mills, Gross, Dobbins, Wallace, Millis & Graesser (2013, *IJAIED*), undergraduates in "trialogues" on research-methods concepts: confusion was induced by staged disagreements/contradictions between animated agents, then asking the learner's opinion. "Contradictions alone did not result in enhanced learning gains"; learning improved only when confusion was actually induced by the contradiction (college population). — [ERIC EJ1190004](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1190004)
- Baker, D'Mello, Rodrigo & Graesser (2010, *Int. J. Human-Computer Studies*), "Better to be frustrated than bored: the incidence, persistence, and impact of learners' cognitive-affective states during interactions with three different computer-based learning environments." Secondary summary: the work leverages "detectors" that infer gaming in log files, built from human observation plus educational data mining; the title reflects the finding that boredom, not frustration, is the state associated with gaming and poorer outcomes. — [Semantic Scholar record (abstract elided)](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1016/j.ijhcs.2009.12.003?fields=title,abstract,year,citationCount); [Global Learning Council summary](https://www.globallearningcouncil.org/posts/gaming-the-system-affect-detectors-and-student-persistence-and-learning-in-computer-based-learning-environments/)
- Baker, "Stupid Tutoring Systems, Intelligent Humans" (IJAIED): gaming = circumventing learning by exploiting system features; detector log patterns include "fast action after errors," "help abuse," "systematic guessing" without genuine attempts, and rapid sequential clicks; gaming is framed as an off-task strategy linked to boredom/disengagement rather than cheating; students who game show lower post-test scores even after controlling prior knowledge; the "Scooter the Tutor" agent gave real-time feedback when gaming was detected. — [Baker IJAIED PDF](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/STS-Baker-IJAIED-v15.pdf). CAUTION: the fetch tool returned specific numbers from this PDF (detector accuracy "60–70%", Scooter reducing gaming "~30–40%", sensor-free affect validity "r ≈ 0.45–0.55") that could not be verified against readable text; treat as unverified.
- Baker et al., "Modeling and Studying Gaming the System with Educational Data Mining" (Springer chapter) — gaming detectors as a documented EDM method. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-4419-5546-3_7)
- Affective AutoTutor (D'Mello, Lehman, Graesser 2011 chapter "A Motivationally Supportive Affect-Sensitive AutoTutor"): between-subjects experiment, 84 learners, two 30-min sessions; affect-sensitive tutor improved learning for low-domain-knowledge learners, especially at deeper comprehension, only during the second session; it was LESS effective for high-knowledge learners in session 1; gains rose from session 1 to 2 with the affective tutor while plateauing with the non-affective one. Secondary summary reports 0.71σ posttest gain for low-knowledge students vs standard AutoTutor. — [Springer chapter](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-4419-9625-1_9); [ResearchGate PDF](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Blair-Lehman/publication/226184026_A_Motivationally_Supportive_Affect-Sensitive_AutoTutor/links/5bffef78299bf1a3c155fe24/A-Motivationally-Supportive-Affect-Sensitive-AutoTutor.pdf); "A Time for Emoting: When Affect-Sensitivity Is and Isn't Effective" — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-642-13388-6_29)
- "Responding to Learners' Cognitive-Affective States with Supportive and Shakeup Dialogues" (D'Mello et al.) — two response families tested: supportive (empathy/encouragement) vs shakeup (challenge/novelty). — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-642-02580-8_65)
- Linguistic confusion detection: Atapattu et al. (arXiv 1903.03286) built "a novel linguistic feature set that is predictive for confusion classification" in learner discourse that outperformed prior models (feature list not in abstract). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.03286)
- Wilson, Shenhav, Straccia & Cohen (2019, *Nature Communications*), "The Eighty Five Percent Rule for optimal learning": for stochastic-gradient-descent-based learners on binary classification, optimal training error is ≈15.87% (≈85% accuracy); authors connect it to the "zone of proximal difficulty." This is a computational/theoretical result, not a human RCT. — [Nature Communications](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-12552-4)
- Bjork's "desirable difficulties" (1994 onward): conditions that feel harder now (spacing, interleaving, retrieval practice) produce more durable learning; the learner's felt fluency is a bad proxy for learning. — [Bjork & Bjork 2020 "Desirable difficulties in theory and practice"](https://www.waddesdonschool.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/Desriable-Difficulties-in-theory-and-practice-Bjork-Bjork-2020.pdf)
- Deslauriers et al. (2019, *PNAS*), large-enrollment intro physics at Harvard: students in active-learning sessions learned more but *felt* they learned less; effort in active learning is misread as poor learning; a polished lecturer inflates the feeling of learning. — [PNAS PDF](https://www.pnas.org/doi/pdf/10.1073/pnas.1821936116); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2019/09/190905171810.htm)

### Inferences
- Chat-app proxies that map onto validated detector features: (a) very short latency after a wrong answer followed by another submit = "fast action after errors"/systematic guessing → gaming/boredom; (b) repeated "give me the answer"/"just tell me"/hint-request chains without an attempt in between = help abuse; (c) long latency plus a hedged or question-form reply ("I think…?", "is it…?") after an impasse = confusion — the good kind, if the tutor then closes it; (d) escalating error count on the same subgoal with rising negativity = unresolved confusion → frustration; (e) flat, minimal, off-topic replies or session abandonment mid-step = boredom/disengagement.
- Response policy by state (from the affect literature): confusion → do NOT immediately dissolve it; give a targeted prompt that lets the student resolve it, then confirm resolution (Lehman 2013 shows gains only when the confusion is real and resolved). Frustration → supportive dialogue plus a smaller next step (Affective AutoTutor gains were for low-knowledge learners once difficulty accumulated). Boredom → "shakeup": raise challenge, change task type, or introduce a contradiction to induce productive confusion.
- Confusion induction is a legitimate tool: a tutor can deliberately present a contradiction (two plausible claims, one wrong) and ask the student to adjudicate — but only pays off when the student actually engages with it.
- Difficulty target: aim for ~80–85% step-level success; consistent ≥95% success indicates the band is too easy (boredom risk); consistent ≤60% with rising latency indicates frustration risk. The 85% figure is model-derived, so treat as a starting heuristic to be tuned empirically.
- Because students misjudge their own learning (Deslauriers 2019; Bjork), self-reported "this is too hard/easy" should be one signal among several, not the controller.
- Affect-adaptive responses help low-knowledge students most and can slightly hurt high-knowledge students early — gate supportive affect responses on estimated mastery, not on a fixed script.

### Gaps
- Exact incidence/persistence percentages from Baker et al. 2010 (e.g., proportion of time in boredom vs confusion, transition probabilities) — publisher abstract elided; PDF host returned an error. Reported qualitatively only.
- Numbers extracted by the fetch tool from the Baker IJAIED PDF (detector accuracy, Scooter effect) are unverified; do not cite as fact.
- No peer-reviewed validation found in this session of specific *chat-app* proxies (hedging words, typing latency) for confusion/frustration with LLM tutors; the linguistic-confusion paper (arXiv 1903.03286) exists but its feature list was not retrievable from the abstract.
- No human RCT validating the 85% rule as a difficulty setpoint for tutoring was found.

## KQ2. Gamification (points/levels/badges/leaderboards) and streaks/daily goals: what helps, what backfires, effect sizes

### Takeaway
Meta-analytic effects of gamification are small-to-medium and most robust for cognitive outcomes (g ≈ 0.49); motivational and behavioral effects are smaller (g ≈ 0.36, 0.25) and less stable under rigorous designs. Leaderboards + badges in a real college course *reduced* intrinsic motivation and exam scores over a semester. Tangible, contingent rewards undermine intrinsic motivation (Deci et al. 1999), whereas informational feedback does not. Streaks raise engagement through loss aversion, and the peer-reviewed evidence says the damage comes at the *break*: broken streaks reduce subsequent engagement, especially when the user blames themselves, and the effect is attenuated when the streak can be repaired. Duolingo's own A/B data confirm that lenience (freezes, weekend amulet) improves retention and that binge use predicts churn.

### Cited Findings
- Sailer & Homner (2020, *Educational Psychology Review* 32:77–112), meta-analysis: gamification effects on cognitive g = 0.49 (95% CI 0.30–0.69; k = 19; N = 1,686), motivational g = 0.36 (CI 0.18–0.54; k = 16; N = 2,246), behavioral g = 0.25 (CI 0.04–0.46; k = 9; N = 951). Cognitive effect stable in the high-methodological-rigor subset; motivational and behavioral effects "less stable." Moderators examined: game fiction, social interaction, learning arrangement, context. — [ERIC EJ1245270](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1245270); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/335189630_The_Gamification_of_Learning_a_Meta-analysis)
- Hamari, Koivisto & Sarsa (2014, HICSS), "Does gamification work?" literature review of empirical studies: gamification produces positive effects but they are "greatly dependent on the context" and on the users; most studies were of low rigor at that time. — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1109/hicss.2014.377); [Aalto](https://research.aalto.fi/en/publications/does-gamification-work-a-literature-review-of-empirical-studies-o/)
- Hanus & Fox (2015, *Computers & Education*), 16-week longitudinal quasi-experiment in two college course sections; gamified section had leaderboard + badges: students in the gamified course reported lower intrinsic motivation, lower satisfaction, and lower final exam scores; intrinsic motivation mediated the exam effect; mechanism = shift to external rewards and social comparison. — [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Assessing-the-effects-of-gamification-in-the-A-on-Hanus-Fox/dff76a9862467d426113ec530f83942016ae3a97); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/265644737_Assessing_the_effects_of_gamification_in_the_classroom_A_longitudinal_study_on_intrinsic_motivation_social_comparison_satisfaction_effort_and_academic_performance)
- Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999, *Psychological Bulletin*), meta-analysis of 128 experiments: engagement-contingent, completion-contingent, and performance-contingent tangible rewards significantly undermined free-choice intrinsic motivation; contradicts Cameron & Pierce (1994). — [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/A-meta-analytic-review-of-experiments-examining-the-Deci-Koestner/8ad9801baea65b40fbbe6fc56e34b2b7be47d0ba); SDT follow-up on education: [Deci, Koestner & Ryan 2001 PDF](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2001_DeciKoestnerRyan.pdf)
- A 2023 meta-analysis (Educational Technology Research and Development): gamification enhances intrinsic motivation and perceptions of autonomy and relatedness but has "minimal impact on competency." — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11423-023-10337-7)
- Silverman & Barasch (2023, *Journal of Consumer Research* 49(6):1095–1117), seven studies: behavioral logs showing an *intact* streak (≥3 consecutive) increase subsequent engagement relative to logs showing a *broken* streak, independent of actual past behavior; the gap is amplified when the break is attributed to oneself, and attenuated when the streak can be "repaired." — [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/49/6/1095/6623414); [INSEAD](https://www.insead.edu/faculty-research/publications/journal-articles/or-track-how-broken-streaks-affect-consumer)
- Duolingo (Kai Herng Loh, 2017, company blog; A/B data, not peer-reviewed): a "streak wager" raised Day-1/7/14 retention, Day-7 by +14%; a "Weekend Amulet" (skip weekends without losing the streak) made users 4% more likely to return a week later and 5% less likely to lose the streak; "by giving learners the option to take a break, they're actually more likely to do more"; users who binge lessons were much more likely to abandon the app than those who paced themselves. — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-streaks-keep-duolingo-learners-committed-to-their-language-goals/)
- Duolingo has run >600 experiments on the streak feature; Streak Freeze reportedly cut churn ~21% among at-risk users (industry summaries, not peer-reviewed). — [Yu-kai Chou streak analysis](https://yukaichou.com/gamification-study/master-the-art-of-streak-design-for-short-term-engagement-and-long-term-success/); [StriveCloud](https://www.strivecloud.io/blog/gamification-examples-boost-user-retention-duolingo)
- "An Analysis of Winning Streak's Effects in Language Course of Duolingo" (2017): streaks boosted motivation/attention, more so for advanced learners than beginners. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/321123886_An_Analysis_of_Winning_Streak's_Effects_in_Language_Course_of_Duolingo)
- IDB working paper "Streaking to Success: The Effects of Highlighting Streaks on Student Effort and Achievement" exists (PDF blocked in this session). — [IDB](https://publications.iadb.org/publications/english/document/Streaking-to-Success-The-Effects-of-Highlighting-Streaks-on-Student-Effort-and-Achievement.pdf)
- Digital-intervention field study in an ed-tech app (arXiv 2310.10850): a contest raised stories completed by 0.96 (+45%) and engagement by 1.09 (+44%) over baseline, and treatment users were 9.8% (2.2 pp) more likely to keep using the app in the four weeks after the contest ended. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.10850)
- Systematic mapping of negative effects of gamification in education software exists (arXiv 2305.08346) — documents practitioner-reported harms. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.08346)

### Inferences
- Use gamification primarily for *cognitive* outcomes (structured practice, retrieval), where the meta-analytic effect holds up; do not rely on it for lasting motivation.
- Avoid leaderboards and comparative badges in a college course context (Hanus & Fox is the most ecologically valid negative result). If any social element is used, make it cooperative (class progress) rather than ranked.
- Points/XP should function as *informational feedback on competence* (what SDT predicts does not undermine), never as tangible contingent rewards for showing up.
- Streak design rules: (1) show intact streaks; (2) provide repair/freeze so a break is attributable to a safety valve rather than the self; (3) count "rest days" or weekly goals rather than strict daily; (4) do not let the streak reward binge sessions — cap daily credit; (5) frame the streak as consistency of practice, not as a loss to protect.
- Levels tied to mastery (competence signal) are safer than levels tied to activity volume.

### Gaps
- Deci et al. 1999 numeric effect sizes (tangible vs verbal rewards) — PubMed blocked by CAPTCHA; only the direction of effects is sourced here.
- No peer-reviewed study of Duolingo streaks with a learning-outcome (not retention) dependent variable found; company blog claims about "learning" are not sourced to an RCT.
- Sailer & Homner's subgroup for adult/college learners specifically was not retrieved.

## KQ3. Growth mindset interventions: effect sizes and replication record

### Takeaway
Best current estimate: brief growth-mindset interventions raise achievement by ~0.05–0.10 SD on average, ~0.16 SD for at-risk/lower-achieving students, with a prediction interval that crosses zero (−0.08 to 0.35). Macnamara & Burgoyne (2023) argue the average is ~0.05 and attributable to design flaws and bias (authors with financial incentives reported larger effects); Burnette et al. (2023) and Tipton et al. (2023) argue the heterogeneity is real and effects concentrate where peer norms support the message. Evidence is overwhelmingly K-12/adolescent; the flagship RCT is ninth-graders.

### Cited Findings
- Yeager et al. (2019, *Nature*) National Study of Learning Mindsets: preregistered RCT, 12,490 ninth-graders in 65 US schools; "a short (less than one hour), online growth mindset intervention" improved grades among lower-achieving students (~0.10 grade points GPA) and increased enrollment in advanced math; "the intervention changed grades when peer norms aligned with the messages of the intervention." — [Semantic Scholar abstract](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1038/s41586-019-1466-y?fields=title,abstract,year); [Atticus Li summary of the study's numbers](https://atticusli.com/replication-crisis/growth-mindset/); mediation follow-up: [PMC9165420](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9165420/)
- Sisk et al. (2018, *Psychological Science*), two meta-analyses: mindset correlates with achievement weakly (N = 365,915; ~1% of variance); intervention effect d = 0.08 (p = .010). — summarized in [Atticus Li](https://atticusli.com/replication-crisis/growth-mindset/) and [ResearchGate M&B paper](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/365261074_Do_Growth_Mindset_Interventions_Impact_Students'_Academic_Achievement_A_Systematic_Review_and_Meta-Analysis_with_Recommendations_for_Best_Practices)
- Macnamara & Burgoyne (2023, *Psychological Bulletin* 149(3–4):133–173), 63 studies, N = 97,672: overall ~0.05 SD; "apparent effects of growth mindset interventions on academic achievement are likely attributable to inadequate study design, reporting flaws, and bias"; authors with a financial incentive reported significantly larger effects. — [CWRU PDF](https://artscimedia.case.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/141/2020/06/26110416/Macnamara-Burgoyne-2023.pdf); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Do-growth-mindset-interventions-impact-students'-A-Macnamara-Burgoyne/873e51943c93c47b23e00e8b0b5e9e9a16492056)
- Burnette et al. (2023, *Psychological Bulletin*): overall 0.09 SD; at-risk/focal groups 0.16 SD; 95% prediction interval −0.08 to 0.35. Tipton et al. (2023) commentary: re-analyzing M&B's own data with multilevel models yields 0.09 SD and the same moderation pattern; M&B's CMA v2 averaging obscured heterogeneity. — [Tipton et al. 2023, PMC10495100](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10495100/)
- Yeager & Dweck (2020) rebuttal to Sisk: argue d ≈ 0.08–0.10 is "acceptably large" for a <1-hour intervention. — via [Atticus Li](https://atticusli.com/replication-crisis/growth-mindset/)

### Inferences
- A mindset message is cheap and low-risk but small; expect ~0.1 SD at best, and only for struggling students in a context that reinforces it. It is not a substitute for difficulty calibration or feedback quality.
- What transfers to a tutor: (a) frame errors as information and effort as the path to competence, consistently, so the *local norm* (the tutor's voice) aligns with the message — the norm-alignment moderator is the one robust finding; (b) target the framing at students showing frustration/repeated errors, not everyone.
- Do not build a standalone "mindset module" and expect measurable outcomes; embed the language in feedback.

### Gaps
- College-specific mindset intervention effect sizes were not found in this session (evidence is K-12/adolescent; Yeager 2019 is ninth grade).
- Exact Yeager 2019 SD-scaled effect (rather than grade points) not retrieved from the primary abstract.

## KQ4. Presenting a teaching step to minimize extraneous load: segmenting/pacing, chunk size, signaling, coherence, modality, diagrams

### Takeaway
Segmenting into learner-paced chunks has a meta-analytic effect of d ≈ 0.45 on transfer when the learner controls pacing vs d ≈ 0.35 when system-paced (Rey et al. 2019, 56 studies/88 comparisons), and helps high-prior-knowledge learners more on retention. Signaling (cues that direct attention to essential elements) gives g ≈ 0.52 retention / 0.31 transfer. Coherence (cutting seductive details) gives small-to-medium retention and medium transfer benefits. Modality (spoken words + graphics rather than on-screen text + graphics) d ≈ 0.72 but is moderated by pacing and element interactivity. The Kestin 2025 Harvard tutor that beat active learning was instructed to answer in "no more than a few sentences" to avoid cognitive overload.

### Cited Findings
- Rey, Beege, Nebel, Wirzberger, Schmitt & Schneider (2019, *Educational Psychology Review*), "A Meta-analysis of the Segmenting Effect": 56 investigations, 88 pairwise comparisons; significant small-to-medium effects on retention and transfer; learner-paced segmenting d = 0.45 on transfer (16 comparisons) vs system-paced d = 0.35 (30 comparisons); segmentation reduces overall cognitive load and increases learning time; learners with high prior knowledge benefited more on retention than low/no prior knowledge learners. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-018-9456-4); [PDF](https://maria-wirzberger.de/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/Rey2019_Article_AMeta-analysisOfTheSegmentingE.pdf)
- Schneider, Beege, Nebel & Rey (2018, *Educational Research Review*), signaling meta-analysis: 95 studies, 11,499 participants; retention g = 0.52 (CI 0.44–0.60, 131 measures), transfer g = 0.31 (CI 0.22–0.40, 67 measures); prior knowledge moderates. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1747938X17300581); [Craig Barton summary](https://tipsforteachers.co.uk/research-a-meta-analysis-of-signaling-principle-in-multimedia-learning-environments/)
- Rey (2012, *Educational Research Review*), seductive-details meta-analysis: 39 experimental effects; small-to-medium effect on retention, medium on transfer, favoring exclusion of interesting-but-irrelevant material. — [ERIC EJ986386](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ986386); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1747938X12000413)
- Ginns (2005, *Learning and Instruction*), modality meta-analysis: 43 independent effects, d = 0.72; element interactivity and pacing moderate the effect. — [ERIC EJ723937](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ723937); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0959475205000459)
- Mayer (2008, *American Psychologist*, "Applying the Science of Learning"): coherence supported in 23/23 tests, median d = 0.86; segmenting 10/10, median d = 0.79; personalization median d = 0.79; (later summaries give modality ≈ 0.72, pre-training ≈ 0.46). — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/23478495_Applying_the_Science_of_Learning_Evidence-Based_Principles_for_the_Design_of_Multimedia_Instruction); Cambridge Handbook chapter on segmenting/pre-training/modality — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-managing-essential-processing-in-multimedia-learning-segmenting-pretraining-and-modality-principles/DD24C2F48B9B1277CE59F78276110258)
- A 2022 systematic review of multimedia principles across learning environments (Smart Learning Environments) confirms principles hold across contexts but with moderators. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-022-00200-2)
- Kestin et al. (2025, *Scientific Reports*), Harvard intro physics RCT: the AI tutor was instructed "to be brief, using no more than a few sentences, to avoid cognitive overload," and students learned significantly more in less time than in an active-learning class, with higher engagement and motivation. — [Nature abstract via Semantic Scholar](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6?fields=title,abstract,year); [Hechinger Report](https://hechingerreport.org/proof-points-ai-tutor-harvard-physics/)
- Subjective cognitive-load questionnaires have been meta-analyzed for reliability/validity (Educational Psychology Review 2022) — self-report load can be measured with a short scale. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-022-09683-4)

### Inferences
- "One reasoning step at a time, student advances" is directly supported: learner-paced segmenting beats system-paced (0.45 vs 0.35). Implement each tutor turn as one segment, ending with an explicit action for the student ("your move"), not auto-advancing.
- Chunk size: a step = one idea + one check. Mayer's segmenting studies use segments of a few sentences / one animation beat; Kestin's tutor cap was "a few sentences." Practical rule: ≤ ~3–5 sentences of explanation per turn before requiring student input.
- Signal: bold or name the one key element in each step (g ≈ 0.5 on retention); avoid decorative asides, jokes, tangents (seductive-details penalty grows with amount).
- Diagram when: the concept is spatial/relational (structures, flows, graphs) AND the diagram is integrated with the words (contiguity) — not as decoration. In a text chat, a diagram costs load unless labeled and referenced in the same message.
- Prior knowledge matters: novices need smaller steps and more pre-training on terms; higher-knowledge students benefit more from segmenting on retention and are hurt by redundant support (expertise reversal). Estimate mastery before choosing step size.
- Modality: a chat tutor cannot use narration; the modality benefit does not apply, so keep text short and put labels on the diagram itself.

### Gaps
- A comprehensive recent per-principle meta-analysis of all Mayer principles (NSF PAR link 10637927) returned 404; per-principle medians above come from Mayer 2008 and individual meta-analyses.
- No study found that directly tests optimal *message length in tokens/sentences* for an LLM tutor; the "few sentences" rule is the design of one successful RCT, not a dose–response result.
- Kestin 2025 effect size (Cohen's d) and N not retrieved from the abstract; secondary sources describe "median learning gains more than double."

## KQ5. Learning styles (meshing hypothesis) and what to use instead

### Takeaway
The meshing hypothesis — instruction matched to a self-reported style (visual/auditory/etc.) improves learning — fails the crossover-interaction test set by Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork (2008); direct tests (Rogowsky 2015/2020; Husmann & O'Loughlin 2019, undergraduate anatomy) found no benefit. A 2024 meta-analysis found a tiny pooled effect (g = 0.31, wide CI) but only ~26% of outcomes showed the required crossover, and the authors advise against adoption. Use prior knowledge, mastery estimates, and the content's nature (spatial vs verbal) to pick representation; offer multimodal representations to everyone.

### Cited Findings
- Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork (2008, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*): the meshing hypothesis requires a crossover interaction (style A learners do better with method A, style B with method B); reviewing the literature they found no adequate evidence; the few well-designed studies found none. — criteria restated in [Clinton-Lisell & Litzinger 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11270031/)
- Rogowsky et al. (2015, 2020): randomly assigned adults/learners to audiobook vs e-text with comprehension tests immediately and two weeks later; no support for meshing. — [Frontiers 2023 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2023.1147498/full)
- Husmann & O'Loughlin (2019, *Anatomical Sciences Education*), undergraduates: VARK-aligned study strategies gave no exam advantage over non-aligned. — [Frontiers 2023 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2023.1147498/full)
- Clinton-Lisell & Litzinger (2024, *Frontiers in Psychology*), meta-analysis: 21 studies, 101 effects, 1,712 participants; pooled g = 0.31 (SE 0.12, CI 0.05–0.57, p = .02); only 26% of outcome measures showed the crossover pattern (25% in 12 further studies); no moderator (design, modality, assessment match, quality) predicted effects; conclusion: benefits "too small and too infrequent to warrant widespread adoption," with "potential for harm through psychological essentialism"; recommend multimodal instruction. — [PMC11270031](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11270031/)
- Belief in learning styles is widespread among educators (pragmatic systematic review, Frontiers in Education 2020). — [Frontiers](https://internal-journal.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feduc.2020.602451/full)
- What works instead: multimodal representation for the content's demands (multimedia principle; signaling g ≈ 0.52; segmenting d ≈ 0.45) and prior-knowledge-based adaptation (segmenting and signaling both moderated by prior knowledge). — [Rey 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-018-9456-4); [Schneider 2018](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1747938X17300581)

### Inferences
- Do not collect a "learning style" onboarding field to drive instruction. If a preference is collected at all, treat it as UI preference (e.g., likes diagrams), not as a pedagogy switch, and never label the student ("you're a visual learner") — essentialism risk.
- Drive representation by content and mastery: spatial/relational content → diagram + short label; procedural content → worked example then faded practice; novice → smaller segments and pre-training on vocabulary.
- Give everyone a dual representation (words + integrated picture) when the content supports it; that is the actual evidence-backed "visual" benefit.

### Gaps
- Pashler et al. 2008 primary text not fetched (paywalled); criteria cited via the 2024 meta-analysis.

## KQ6. Autonomy vs system steering (SDT, choice, learner control) and goal setting / implementation intentions

### Takeaway
SDT: intrinsic and identified motivation predict success and persistence (ρ ≈ .11–.13 for objective outcomes, ≈ .29–.32 self-report); introjected/ego motives buy persistence at the cost of ill-being. Choice boosts intrinsic motivation and effort (Patall 2008, 41 studies), but *learner control of instructional sequencing* in ed-tech has ~zero effect on learning (g = 0.05, Karich 2014). So: give autonomy over goals, order of topics, pacing, and low-stakes options; let the system steer difficulty, step size, and hinting. Implementation intentions ("if X, then I will Y") raise goal attainment d = 0.65; MCII raised practice-problem completion by ~60% in adolescents and increased study time in university students.

### Cited Findings
- Howard, Bureau, Guay, Chong & Ryan (2021, *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 16(6):1300–1323), meta-analysis of 344 samples, 223,209 participants, 26 outcomes: intrinsic motivation relates to success and well-being; identified regulation (personal value) is most strongly related to persistence; introjected regulation predicts persistence and performance goals but also ill-being; effects on objective outcomes are smaller (identified ρ = .11, intrinsic ρ = .13) than on self-report (ρ = .29, .32). — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1745691620966789); [preprint PDF](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/2021_HowardBureauGuayChongRyan_Student-Motivation_PrePrint.pdf)
- Bureau, Howard et al. (2021), "Pathways to Student Motivation": meta-analysis of antecedents (autonomy support, competence support, relatedness) of motivation types. — [PDF](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/2021_PathwaystoStudentMotiv_BureaHowardEtal.pdf); Howard, Slemp & Wang (2024) meta-analysis of need satisfaction — [PDF](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/2024_HowardSlempWang_Meta.pdf)
- Patall, Cooper & Robinson (2008, *Psychological Bulletin* 134:270–300), 41 studies, children and adults: providing choice enhanced intrinsic motivation, effort, task performance, and perceived competence. — [ERIC EJ787695](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ787695); [PDF](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/2008_PatallCooperRobinson_PsychBulletin.pdf)
- Karich, Burns & Maki (2014, *Review of Educational Research*), updated meta-analysis of learner control within educational technology: 18 studies, 29 effects; overall g = 0.05 (near zero). — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/0034654314526064); [ERIC EJ1034262](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1034262)
- Learner-paced segmentation (learner controls when to advance) does help: d = 0.45 vs 0.35 system-paced (Rey 2019). — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-018-9456-4)
- Deslauriers et al. (2019, *PNAS*): students prefer and rate more highly the passive format from which they learn less. — [PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/pdf/10.1073/pnas.1821936116)
- Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006, *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 38:69–119): 94 independent tests; implementation intentions raise goal attainment d = 0.65 (CI 0.6–0.7). — [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Implementation-intentions-and-goal-achievement:-A-Gollwitzer-Sheeran/c4deb3507fe725ce6363c1735f1ba83bab20d665); [KOPS](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/handle/123456789/10973)
- Duckworth, Grant, Loew, Oettingen & Gollwitzer (2011, *Educational Psychology*), high-school students preparing for the PSAT: MCII (mental contrasting + implementation intentions) group completed >60% more practice questions (140 vs 84) than controls. — [ERIC EJ911106](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ911106)
- MCII increases study time for university students (2020/2021 study). — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/347630081_Mental_contrasting_with_implementation_intentions_increases_study_time_for_university_students); MCII meta-analysis (2021, PMC) — [PMC8149892](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8149892/)
- Duolingo: users who binge were more likely to abandon; paced use predicted retention (company data). — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-streaks-keep-duolingo-learners-committed-to-their-language-goals/)

### Inferences
- Autonomy surface: let the student choose *what* (topic, assignment, which concept to attack), *when to advance* (learner-paced steps), and *how to engage* (quiz me / explain / work an example). Withhold control of *difficulty* and *hint depth* from the default path — learner control of sequencing yields no learning benefit and students systematically prefer the lower-effort path.
- Offer a few meaningful choices (Patall's moderator literature: a small number of instructionally relevant options works better than many; exact moderator numbers not retrieved here).
- Competence support: mastery-referenced feedback (progress on a concept) rather than normative comparison; this is the SDT-consistent form of "points."
- Relatedness with an AI tutor is the weakest SDT leg; see KQ7 for peer features.
- Goal setting: at session start, elicit a concrete if-then plan ("When I open Sapling after dinner, I'll do 10 minutes on X"), and at session end re-elicit for next time. Expect real behavioral gains (d ≈ 0.65 on goal attainment across domains; ~60% more practice in the one direct study).

### Gaps
- Patall 2008 numeric effect size and moderators (number of choices, instructionally relevant vs irrelevant) — PubMed and SDT PDF both unreadable in this session.
- No RCT found directly comparing student-steered vs system-steered *LLM* tutoring on learning; the learner-control meta-analysis predates LLM tutors.

## KQ7. Peers and class-level features alongside a 1:1 AI tutor (peer instruction, social presence)

### Takeaway
Active learning in undergraduate STEM raises exam performance by 0.47 SD and cuts failure from 34% to 22% (Freeman 2014, 225 studies). Peer Instruction doubled Mazur's normalized gain (0.25 → 0.49 the first year, 0.74 by year six), and peer discussion improves performance on isomorphic questions even when no one in the group knew the answer (Smith 2009). Pedagogical-agent "social presence" yields only small learning effects and is smaller for post-secondary than K-12 students. A well-designed AI tutor beat an active-learning classroom on learning gains in one Harvard RCT — so peers are not required for learning gains, but they supply relatedness and the discussion mechanism that an AI cannot replicate alone.

### Cited Findings
- Freeman et al. (2014, *PNAS*): 225 studies, undergraduate STEM; exam/concept-inventory performance +0.47 SD under active learning (158 studies); odds ratio for failing under traditional lecture 1.95 (67 studies); failure rate 34% → 22%; exam scores ~+6%. — [PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/abs/10.1073/pnas.1319030111); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24821756/)
- Crouch & Mazur (2001, *American Journal of Physics* 69(9):970): normalized gain 0.25 in the semester before PI (Hake "traditional" range), 0.49 the first PI semester, rising to 0.74 by the sixth implementation; gains in conceptual reasoning AND quantitative problem solving. — [AJP](https://pubs.aip.org/aapt/ajp/article/69/9/970/310529/Peer-Instruction-Ten-years-of-experience-and); [PDF](https://web.mit.edu/jbelcher/www/TEALref/Crouch_Mazur.pdf)
- Smith, Wood, Adams, Wieman, Knight, Guild & Su (2009, *Science* 323:122–124), undergraduate genetics: peer discussion improved performance on isomorphic follow-up questions (77% correct); 44% answered incorrectly before discussion then correctly after; gains occurred even when no student in the group initially knew the answer — attributed to conceptual gains, not peer influence. — [Science](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1165919); [PMC summary](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3140391/)
- Smith et al. (2011, *CBE-LSE*): combining peer discussion with instructor explanation beats either alone. — [CBE-LSE](https://www.lifescied.org/doi/10.1187/cbe.10-08-0101)
- Vickrey et al. (2015, *CBE-LSE*), research-based implementation of Peer Instruction literature review. — [CBE-LSE](https://www.lifescied.org/doi/10.1187/cbe.14-11-0198)
- Deslauriers et al. (2019, *PNAS*): active learning → more learning, lower felt learning; students need to be told this to persist. — [PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/pdf/10.1073/pnas.1821936116)
- Kestin et al. (2025, *Scientific Reports*): college students learned "significantly more in less time" with a pedagogically designed AI tutor than in an active-learning class, and reported higher engagement and motivation; control was active learning, not lecture. — [Semantic Scholar abstract](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6?fields=title,abstract,year); [Hechinger Report](https://hechingerreport.org/proof-points-ai-tutor-harvard-physics/)
- Schroeder, Adesope & Barouch Gilbert (2013, *J. Educational Computing Research*): 43 studies, 3,088 participants; pedagogical agents produce a small but significant learning effect; larger for K-12 than post-secondary; text-communicating agents beat narrating agents. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.2190/EC.49.1.a)
- Castro-Alonso et al. (2021), 21 studies: pedagogical agents improve learning vs none; human voice > machine voice; larger effects for STEM. — via [Frontiers 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2024.1416033/full)
- Instructor-present videos meta-analysis (2022): effects on learning, load, motivation, social presence quantified (Education and Information Technologies). — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-022-11154-w)
- Sailer & Homner 2020 examined social interaction as a gamification moderator (see KQ2). — [ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1245270)

### Inferences
- A "room"/class feature should replicate the PI mechanism, not just chat: (1) each student commits to an answer alone (to the AI); (2) students see the distribution; (3) short peer discussion; (4) re-vote; (5) AI or instructor explanation. Smith 2009 implies value even when everyone is initially wrong, so the feature works for hard concepts.
- Relatedness (the SDT need the 1:1 AI serves least) is best met by peers and class-level progress, not by making the AI more "human" — agent social presence gives small gains and less for college students.
- Since active learning feels worse than it is, the room/AI should say so explicitly ("this feels harder because it works") — Deslauriers 2019 showed telling students this improved acceptance.
- Class-level features must not become leaderboards (Hanus & Fox); use aggregate progress, shared misconceptions, or "what the class got wrong" as the social signal.

### Gaps
- No RCT found combining a 1:1 LLM tutor with a peer-instruction layer; the peer evidence is classroom-based.
- Numeric effect sizes for the Schroeder 2013 and Castro-Alonso 2021 meta-analyses were not retrieved (only "small but significant").

## KQ8. Over-reliance and metacognitive laziness with AI tutors; designing against it

### Takeaway
Two RCT-grade results converge: unrestricted ChatGPT-style help raises in-task performance and then leaves learners no better (Fan 2024/25: better essay scores, no knowledge-gain or transfer difference, SRL centered on the tool) or worse (Bastani 2025: −0.19 SD on the unassisted exam for "GPT Base" vs −0.01 SD for a guardrailed "GPT Tutor" that gives teacher-designed hints instead of answers). Guardrails that withhold final answers and force the student's own attempt remove the harm; a pedagogically designed tutor (Kestin 2025) can then beat active learning.

### Cited Findings
- Fan, Tang, Le, Shen, Tan, Zhao, Li, Gašević & Chen (2024 online / 2025, *British Journal of Educational Technology*), "Beware of metacognitive laziness": 117 university students; four conditions — ChatGPT, human expert, writing-analytics tool, no extra tool; no difference in post-task intrinsic motivation; significant differences in frequency and sequences of SRL processes; ChatGPT group improved essay scores most but knowledge gain and transfer did not differ; ChatGPT group's SRL "centred on ChatGPT" while human teachers triggered more metacognitive transitions (orientation ↔ evaluation); authors warn of "dependence on technology" and "metacognitive laziness." — [Wiley](https://bera-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/bjet.13544); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09315)
- Bastani, Bastani, Sungu, Ge, Kabakcı & Mariman (2025, *PNAS*), "Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning": ~1,000 high-school math students (K-12); GPT Base (ChatGPT-like) vs GPT Tutor (prompted with teacher-designed hints, no direct answers); practice performance +48% (Base) and +127% (Tutor); on the subsequent unassisted exam, Base −0.19 SD vs control, Tutor −0.01 SD; students used base GPT-4 as a "crutch." — [PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2422633122); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12232635/)
- Kestin et al. (2025): AI tutor designed with pedagogical best practices (brief responses, scaffolding, not giving answers away) outperformed active learning with college students. — [Semantic Scholar abstract](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6?fields=title,abstract,year)
- Deslauriers 2019 / Bjork: felt fluency ≠ learning; AI that makes things feel easy will be preferred and will under-teach. — [PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/pdf/10.1073/pnas.1821936116)
- "RelianceScope" (arXiv 2602.16251) — an analytical framework for measuring students' reliance on GenAI chatbots in problem solving (2026 preprint, not peer-reviewed). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.16251)
- "The Effortless Trap: Productive Struggle, AI, and the Illusion of Learning" (arXiv 2606.26181, preprint). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.26181)

### Inferences
- Hard rule: never emit the final answer to a graded/assigned problem on first request; give the next hint and require an attempt. This is the single design difference between −0.19 SD and −0.01 SD in Bastani.
- Build metacognitive prompts into the loop (the transitions human tutors trigger): before a step, ask for a plan/prediction; after, ask for self-evaluation ("which step were you least sure of?"). Fan's data show the AI condition lacked these orientation↔evaluation transitions.
- Instrument reliance: count hint requests per attempt, answer-request frequency, and copy-paste-like turns; use as a signal for the gaming/help-abuse detector in KQ1.
- Periodic unassisted checks (closed-book mini-quizzes) are the only honest measure of learning; in-session performance with the tutor is inflated by 48–127%.
- Add "productive struggle" framing so the student tolerates delayed answers (Deslauriers-style explanation of why it feels worse).

### Gaps
- Bastani is K-12 (high school); no equivalent-scale college RCT with a guardrailed vs unguardrailed LLM found.
- Fan et al. effect sizes for SRL differences not retrieved (abstract gives direction only).
