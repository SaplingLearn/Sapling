# Keep the guardrails, rebuild the measuring stick

> **Correction (2026-11-07, implementation pass):** this report calls the `|R − 0.33|` review ordering a misattribution to DASH. That is wrong: Lindsey, Shroyer, Pashler & Mozer (2014) selected the items whose predicted recall was nearest 0.33, a value from the Khajah, Lindsey & Mozer (2013) simulations. The ordering stays; `fsrs.order_due` now cites both. Every other recommendation is unchanged. Implemented items are spec §13 A110.

Sapling's core bet is the right one. The strongest 2025–2026 evidence says an LLM tutor helps or harms depending on **who generates the answer**. Unrestricted or answer-giving AI raises practice scores and lowers later unassisted performance: −17% on a closed-book exam in Bastani's high-school RCT and **−0.37 SD in final grades in a 2,379-student US university trial** whose tutor was left in "direct instruction" mode. Answer-withholding, solution-grounded, attempt-first designs avoid that harm but have not yet shown durable gains. Sapling's answer-withholding H0–H6 ladder, its rule that only graded checks count as mastery evidence, its classical BKT-plus-FSRS learner model, and its delayed tool-free post-test are the evidence-aligned choices, and the audit supports keeping them. The weak point is measurement. Every number the system relies on comes from one pipeline: items an LLM generated from classmates' uploads, keys taken from peer material, scores from an LLM grader, and a post-test drawn from that same item pool after only two days. That pipeline is unvalidated, and the literature shows exactly how it inflates apparent learning. The foundations audit also demotes several constants Sapling cites, including Wilson's 85% rule, ZPD success-rate bands, Bloom's 2σ and Kestin's 0.73–1.3 SD, from evidence to tunable hypotheses. The recommended direction is therefore to **keep the pedagogy, make the outcome measures independent of the treatment, validate the items and grader against humans, and run a preregistered within-student, concept-randomized trial**. That trial can detect a realistic 0.2–0.3 SD effect with about 120–200 students. No college RCT has yet shown a positive unassisted effect from a student-facing LLM tutor at a delay of a month or more, so Sapling would be filling a real gap, not repeating a known result.

## Three pillars hold, four constants collapse

Re-checking the studies Sapling relies on gives an uneven picture. Some pillars are among the most replicated findings in education research. Others turn out to be theory, single-site results, or misreadings. The table gives the verdict for each. Text after the table explains the verdicts that change design decisions.

| Foundation | What Sapling uses it for | Verdict after audit | Key reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bastani et al. 2025 (PNAS) | Justifies answer withholding | **Solid on direction**, qualified on size | Independently corroborated in college (Liu et al. 2026, −0.37 SD); guarded tutor avoided harm but produced no exam gain |
| Kestin et al. 2025 (Sci Rep) | Design template, effect-size anchor | **Shaky as a magnitude**, fine as proof of concept | Single site, n≈194, immediate test only, no replication |
| Bloom 2σ | Implicit ambition | **Refuted as a benchmark** | Two short dissertations with a stricter 90% mastery criterion; replication d = 0.52 |
| Wilson 85% rule | Success-rate targets | **Shaky for college learning** | Derived for gradient-descent learners on binary perceptual tasks; no human experiment located |
| ZPD as a success-rate band | Phase bands | **Shaky**; assistance-gap version qualified | No validation of a % band; dynamic assessment measures help needed |
| Expertise reversal (Tetzlaff 2025 meta) | Ladder ceilings by band | **Solid, asymmetric** | 60 experiments, N = 5,924; helping novices matters more than withholding from experts |
| Productive failure (Sinha & Kapur 2021) | Attempt-first probe | **Qualified**, g ≈ 0.36 | Conditional on fidelity, older learners, conceptual outcomes |
| Mastery learning | 0.98 mastery gate | **Qualified** | ≈0.5 SD on local tests, ≈0.08 on standardized tests |
| BKT vs modern KT | Learner model | **BKT defensible at small n** | Logistic/IRT-style models match or beat deep KT outside large datasets |
| FSRS-6 | Review scheduling | **Best interpretable recall model, flashcards only** | No evidence on conceptual items that change each review |
| Retrieval, spacing, successive relearning | Review phase | **Solid** | Classroom g ≈ 0.50; spaced vs massed g ≈ 0.74 |

### Bastani holds, and the college replication is harsher

The Bastani PNAS trial gave about 1,000 Turkish high-school students one of three conditions: a ChatGPT-like interface, a teacher-designed hint-only "GPT Tutor", or no access. Base access cut the later unassisted exam by **17%**, and the guarded tutor "largely mitigated" the harm ([PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2422633122)). The direction now has independent college confirmation. Liu et al.'s October 2026 EdWorkingPaper randomized 30 instructors and 2,379 undergraduates to a GPT-4o study assistant that used RAG over course materials. Final grades fell **0.27–0.37 SD**, LMS participation fell **0.90 SD**, and first-generation students lost more than twice as much as continuing-generation students ([EdWorkingPapers 26-1598](https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai26-1598.pdf)). Two details make this study directly relevant to Sapling. First, nearly all instructors kept the default "direct instruction" mode, and only two ended the term in tutor mode. Second, RAG grounding in course material did not prevent the harm. The paper is a non-peer-reviewed working paper with few clusters and only 14.6% uptake, so the mechanism is partly speculative: the authors suggest instructor encouragement shifted students' broader GenAI use. A PNAS correction to Bastani was issued on 2025-08-20 ([Correction](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2518204122)), and its content could not be read (403). Check it before quoting Bastani's figures externally.

The weaker half of Bastani matters as much as the strong half. The guarded tutor **avoided harm but did not beat control** on the exam. The best-designed newer trial reinforces this. Oreopoulos, Liut, Sungu and Low randomized more than 6,000 middle-schoolers to AI versus no AI and to mastery versus non-mastery progression. Mastery progression "did not by itself improve delayed learning". AI embedded in the mastery workflow gave only **marginally significant** gains on a one-week delayed test ([NBER w35621](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w35621/w35621.pdf)). Guardrails are therefore necessary but not shown to be sufficient.

### Effect-size anchors should be retired

Kestin et al. reported median post-test scores of 4.5 versus 3.5 and effects of 0.73–1.3 SD for a Harvard physics AI tutor over in-class active learning ([Sci Rep](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-97652-6)). The test was immediate, aligned to the lesson, and possibly at ceiling; the study ran at one site with a developer-built tool, and no replication exists ([erctpapers summary](https://erctpapers.com/papers/47-kestin-et-al-2025-ai-tutoring-active-learning.html)). The design is worth copying: instructor-written step-by-step solutions in the prompt, short scaffolded turns, and no wholesale answers. The magnitude is not a target. Bloom's 2σ is worse. It came from two roughly three-week University of Chicago dissertations, in which the tutored group also worked to a **90% mastery criterion versus 80%**, and a replication found d = 0.52 ([Nintil](https://nintil.com/bloom-sigma/)). The benchmark that should govern Sapling's expectations is Kraft's. Across 747 RCTs, the median effect on standardized outcomes is about 0.10 SD, and **preregistered IES-funded trials have a median of 0.03 SD** ([Kraft 2020](https://www.matthewakraft.com/s/Kraft-2020-Interpreting-Effect-Sizes-ER.pdf)). Lortie-Forgues and Inglis found a mean effect of **0.06 SD** across 141 large education RCTs ([White Rose](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/141754/)). A durable, transferable effect of 0.1–0.2 SD would be a strong result for Sapling, not a disappointment.

### The 85% rule and ZPD bands are hypotheses, not findings

Wilson et al.'s optimal error rate of about 15.87% is a mathematical result for stochastic-gradient learners on two-choice tasks with Gaussian noise. It was demonstrated in simulations, and the lead author expected it to apply mainly to perceptual learning ([Nat Commun](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-12552-4); [Neuroscience News](https://neurosciencenews.com/learning-optimized-15171/)). No human experimental confirmation was located. Desirable-difficulties research suggests the success rate that is optimal for durable learning sits below the rate that is optimal for in-session acquisition. That logic supports Sapling's move to **phase-specific bands**: probe 0.50–0.62, assisted acquisition 0.75–0.90, unassisted practice 0.65–0.85. It does not support citing literature for the specific numbers. The ZPD has no empirical operationalization as a success-rate band at all. The defensible measurable proxy comes from dynamic assessment: the gap between unassisted and assisted success, or the lowest hint rung a student needs. Dynamic-assessment ZPD measures did predict later school performance in children ([Luković et al.](https://scidar.kg.ac.rs/bitstream/123456789/16069/4/0048-57052100004L.pdf)). Sapling already logs the rung needed on every attempt, so it can validate its own operationalization against delayed outcomes.

### Expertise reversal and productive failure support the ladder's shape, with one correction

Tetzlaff, Simonsmeier, Peters and Brod's 2025 meta-analysis (60 experiments, 176 effects) finds the expertise-reversal effect robust. It also finds it **asymmetric**: "providing novices with assistance has a stronger effect than withholding assistance from experts" ([pedocs](https://www.pedocs.de/frontdoor.php?la=en&source_opus=34113&nr=1&anker=start&zeitschriftentitel=Learning+and+instruction&zeitschriftenjahr=2025&zeitschriftenband=98)). Sapling's band-dependent ceilings already follow the main effect: worked example first for novices without the prerequisite, and attempt-first for proficient learners. The asymmetry adds a rule. When the BKT estimate is uncertain or rests on few observations, the cost of under-helping is larger than the cost of over-helping, so the policy should resolve ties toward more scaffolding and fade it, not withhold it. Productive failure supports the attempt-first probe for conceptual topics with college learners. Sinha and Kapur report g ≈ 0.36 [0.20, 0.51] on conceptual knowledge and transfer, with no cost to procedural knowledge ([PDF](https://janfasen.nl/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Sinha-and-Kapur-PS-I.pdf)). Their bias-"corrected" g of 0.87 is implausible and should not be quoted. The effect depends on design fidelity and on learner age ([Fermi News](https://news.fermi.ai/research/productive-failure-meta-analysis)), which is consistent with Sapling gating attempt-first on prerequisite knowledge.

## Who generates the answer decides whether an LLM tutor helps

Across every rigorous 2024–2026 field study in the notes, the sign of the unassisted or delayed effect tracks one variable: **whether the student can use the AI to produce the answer**.

On the harmful side, the pattern repeats across settings. A Chinese county panel of 26,811 secondary students found homework scores up 18% and monthly closed-book exams down 20% within six months. The losses were concentrated among apparent "outsourcers", and students who kept their homework time unchanged saw small losses ([VoxEU](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/generative-ai-learning-penalty-secondary-school)). A McGraw Hill analysis of ALEKS data estimated that College Algebra study time fell **26.9%** and that the odds of answering AI-susceptible proctored items correctly fell to **0.75×** ([arXiv 2605.21629](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.21629)). The authors are vendor-affiliated and the paper is a preprint. In a 120-undergraduate RCT, ChatGPT study produced 57.5% correct on a 45-day retention test, against 68.5% for traditional study ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2590291125010186)). Lehmann et al. found that generating solutions with an LLM deepened coverage but made understanding shallower, while asking it for explanations deepened understanding. They also found that LLMs **widened the gap between low- and high-prior-knowledge students** ([arXiv 2409.09047](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047)).

On the positive side, every study that worked controlled what the model could say and grounded it in a solution an expert had checked. Bastani's GPT Tutor was prompted with teacher solutions. Kestin's tutor used instructor-written steps. Pardos and Bhandari found that vetted ChatGPT hints matched human-authored hints, while about 32% of unvetted hints in earlier work contained errors ([PLOS ONE](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0304013)). Google's LearnLM trial with Eedi had an expert tutor approve each draft: 76.4% were sent with minimal edits, and students solved 66.2% of novel later-topic problems against 60.7% with human tutors alone ([arXiv 2512.23633](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.23633)). That was an exploratory vendor preprint with 165 students. Human-in-the-loop designs have the cleanest positive evidence: Tutor CoPilot raised exit-ticket mastery 4 percentage points overall and 9 points for students of weaker tutors ([arXiv 2410.03017](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.03017)). Grounding in course material is not enough on its own. The RAG tutor in Liu et al. still harmed grades, and a reported semester RCT of a RAG chatbot by Thoeni and Fryer (known only second-hand) found no effect.

Three implications follow for Sapling. First, the H0–H6 ladder, the genuine-attempt gate, and the leak detector are the product's most evidence-backed features. Sapling's references, however, come from **classmates' shared material, not instructor-verified solutions**, which is weaker than every positive study used. Second, uptake is the dominant real-world failure. Only 14.6% of Liu's treated students ever used the tool, and 73.8% of their requests sought information or solutions while 0.7% sought feedback on their own work. In the two-year Khanmigo trial, 96% of students tried the tool, but the median student messaged it on only a third of practice days, mostly with bare answers ([NBER w35620](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35620)). The loop needs to sit inside required work, such as assigned practice, mastery gates and scheduled reviews, rather than wait as optional chat. Third, Liu et al. found that an AI tutor can lower grades by displacing existing study. Sapling should log total study time and course-platform activity alongside in-tool metrics, and track first-generation and low-prior-knowledge subgroups explicitly.

The head-to-head question remains open. The one randomized comparison of a Socratic and a non-Socratic LLM found **no learning difference**, and students rated the Socratic version less helpful ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/386547103_The_Impact_of_Large_Language_Models_on_Students_A_Randomised_Study_of_Socratic_vs_Non-Socratic_AI_and_the_Role_of_Step-by-Step_Reasoning_Statements_and_Declarations)); its peer-review status is unverified. Given Lehmann's complement-versus-substitute result, the evidence supports **short explanations followed by a student attempt** over pure questioning. That matches Sapling's five-sentence, one-question turn shape. The cap itself has no direct validation: no study varies LLM turn length.

## Graded checks beat every softer signal Sapling has been tempted by

Most of the contested features share one temptation: treating something the student says or feels as evidence of learning. The literature is consistent that this is a mistake. Some of the features are still worth adding, but as learning activities, not as evidence.

### "Feels obviously true" is the wrong stopping rule

Deslauriers et al. randomized intro physics students at Harvard. Students in active-learning classes **learned more but felt they learned less** than students in polished lectures, and the authors warn that relying on student perceptions steers instruction toward weaker methods ([PDF](https://openlab.citytech.cuny.edu/gamelab/files/2023/01/Deslauriersa-et-al-2019.pdf)). Bastani adds an AI-specific version of the same effect: Base access lifted assisted practice and lowered the unassisted exam ([Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/without-guardrails-generative-ai-can-harm-education)). Advancement should stay tied to generating the step on a graded check, as Sapling already requires. The tutor can also tell students early that effort feels like failure, as Deslauriers recommends.

### Predict-before-explain works per segment, not per step

Pretesting is robust. Pan and Carpenter's review of more than 60 articles finds that prequestions "can often enhance learning" even when early answers are mostly wrong, **provided the correct answer follows** ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-023-09814-5)). ChatGPT-generated prequestions matched human-made ones in four experiments ([Pan et al. 2025](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/PSTIW_2025.pdf)). Adaptive AI pretests were more engaging but produced no better final scores than fixed ones ([arXiv 2504.10249](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.10249v1)). The boundary matters. For comprehension of conceptual text, explanation training beat Predict-Observe-Explain training ([EasyChair](https://easychair.org/publications/preprint/tDPk)), and no study tests prediction at every reasoning step. Sapling's probe is a placement instrument. One ungraded prequestion at the start of each segment, followed promptly by the explanation, is a supported addition. A per-step prediction requirement is not, and it would break the one-question-per-turn rule.

### Thinking fields: prompt self-explanation, but do not grade it as mastery

Prompted self-explanation is among the best-supported interventions, at **g = 0.55**; interrogative prompts give g = 0.56 and imperative prompts 0.40 ([Bisra et al. 2018](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2018-bisra.pdf)). The benefit comes from the student generating the explanation, not from the tutor reading it, and the studies used prompts at key junctures rather than mandatory free text at every step. VanLehn's review found that interaction finer than the step adds little ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2011.611369)). Using that free text as diagnostic data is where the danger lies. Classifying misconceptions in wrong-answer explanations works on curated benchmarks. Detecting hidden misconceptions behind correct answers produces roughly **4 false alarms per true detection** ([arXiv 2606.23205](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.23205)), and a workshop paper reports positive predictive value of **10.9%** ([CEUR](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-4282/short7.pdf)). At that base rate, acting on a flagged misconception without a confirming check misleads the student most of the time. The right use is targeted "why does this follow?" prompts as the turn's single question, with any LLM reading of the answer used only to choose the next probe item.

### Confidence ratings change what they measure

Ingendahl, Halamish and Undorf's 2025 *Psychological Bulletin* meta-analysis (344 effects, N = 15,079) finds that judgments of learning change memory: on average g = 0.22, but negative for some materials, with publication bias. The authors conclude JOLs are "not a neutral measurement tool" ([Bar-Ilan](https://education.biu.ac.il/en/node/12525)). Confidence earns its place through hypercorrection: confidently wrong errors are corrected more readily after feedback, at 85% versus 61% for short-answer items in one applied study, though not for true/false items ([ASHS](https://ashs.confex.com/ashs/2017/webprogramarchives/Paper26571.html); [PMC4679660](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4679660/)). Sapling's rule that confidence is used for diagnosis only, and that confident errors are routed to misconception confrontation, is well aligned with this. A coarse three-point rating on graded checks is supported. A slider on every answer is not.

### LLM knowledge tracing belongs behind a classical model

The 2025–2026 consensus is to use the LLM as an annotator and a classical model as the tracer. Scarlatos, Baker and Lan showed that GPT-4o can label dialogue turns by knowledge component and correctness with expert-judged accuracy ([arXiv 2409.16490](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.16490v1)). Used as the tracer, raw LLMs fail. On FoundationalASSIST, 70–120B models "barely achieve a trivial baseline", and their item discrimination is below chance ([arXiv 2602.00070](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.00070)). Fine-tuned LLM tracers stayed about 6% below DKT on AUC and produced **inconsistent and wrong-direction mastery updates** ([arXiv 2512.23036](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.23036)). Sapling's design, in which the LLM grades and BKT traces, is therefore correct. One detail deserves review. The `chat_turn` channel (G = S = 0.30) still moves the BKT belief even though it never counts toward the strong-observation gate. Pelánek and Řihák found that the choice of evidence sources and thresholds matters more for mastery decisions than the model family ([MUNI](https://is.muni.cz/publication/1392193/en/Experimental-Analysis-of-Mastery-Learning-Criteria/Pelanek-Rihak)), which favours routing chat signals only to scheduling and probe selection.

### Knowledge graphs and cue variation: keep the prerequisite graph, vary the checks

The concept-map evidence covers learners *building* maps (g = 0.72) or studying them (g = 0.43) ([SFU Summit](https://summit.sfu.ca/item/17789)). It says nothing in favour of a system silently extracting proposition or reasoning-chain graphs from chat, which would inherit the extraction-reliability problem above and add another store of sensitive learner data. Sapling's prerequisite graph is the standard ITS pattern and should stay. An extracted proposition graph should not be built unless it is low-trust and never writes mastery. Cue variation is better supported. Interleaving gives g = 0.42 overall but **only 0.34 for math**, nothing for expository text, and harm for word lists (g = −0.39). It helps most when categories are confusable ([PubMed 31556629](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31556629/)). Pan and Rickard find that retrieval practice transfers mainly when supportive moderators are present ([PDF](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/PR_2018.pdf)). Sapling's generator can produce surface-varied isomorphs and interleave confusable concepts within a course in review queues, and should gate multi-concept items on mastery of their components.

## The learner model is sound; its constants are nominal until calibrated

BKT is a defensible choice at Sapling's scale of tens of students per course. Gervet, Koedinger, Schneider and Mitchell found that well-featured logistic regression tends to win on moderate-sized datasets and deep KT wins on large ones ([JEDM](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/download/451/123)). On ASSISTments 2012, an attention model's calibration error was about forty times a restricted gradient-boosted model's (0.0236 versus 0.0006) while AUC barely moved ([arXiv 2609.28502](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.28502), preprint). The upgrades the evidence points to are an item-difficulty term (IRT-style), which matters because LLM-generated items vary in difficulty, and logging predictions against next outcomes to measure calibration. The 0.98 threshold has some support: on Rori data, 0.98 gave "additional benefits" over 0.95 in the next lesson ([EDM 2025](https://educationaldatamining.org/edm2025/proceedings/2025.EDM.short-papers.4/2025.EDM.short-papers.4.pdf)), and a randomized JEDM study found higher thresholds reduced forgetting, with the advantage shrinking over time ([JEDM 900](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/download/900/254)). Rori is K-12 foundational math, though, and with hand-set guess and slip values "0.98" is a nominal number. The requirement of three strong unassisted observations is the more robust safeguard. Channel noise should be estimated rather than assumed. LLM–human agreement on university short answers was only QWK 0.585–0.640 and fell on higher-order items ([JCAL 2026 record](https://gcris.yasar.edu.tr/entities/publication/1eeab52d-b73f-42b4-baa8-f2c94edfc8c1/full)), which suggests the teach-back slip of 0.20 is optimistic.

Scheduling evidence is strong for the principle and weak for precision. Classroom quizzing improves learning by about g = 0.50 ([HKBU](https://scholars.hkbu.edu.hk/en/publications/testing-quizzing-boosts-classroom-learning-a-systematic-and-meta-/)). Spaced retrieval beats massed retrieval by about g = 0.74, but **expanding versus uniform intervals differ by only g ≈ 0.03** ([ACU summary](https://inspire.acu.edu.au/articles/spaced-practice)). Both figures come from secondary summaries. Successive relearning improved intro-psychology exam scores by roughly a letter grade, but its advantage shrank to **d = 0.28 for probability problem-solving** ([Dunlosky et al. guide](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-successive-relearning-dunlosky-greve-badali-wissman-rawson.pdf); [ERIC EJ1263194](https://searchworks.jioinstitute.edu.in/articles/eric__EJ1263194)). On the open benchmark of about 350 million Anki reviews, FSRS-6 is the best non-neural model: log loss 0.346, against 0.368 for DASH and 0.469 for HLR. When same-day reviews are included, **DASH beats FSRS-6** (0.349 versus 0.384) ([srs-benchmark](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark)). Every review in that benchmark is a flashcard. Sapling schedules concepts and generates a fresh item at each review, so part of FSRS's "difficulty" will reflect item variance rather than memory. Per-user optimization will not converge on a semester's data, and default parameters perform worse than optimized ones. Two corrections follow from this. Same-day relearning steps should stay fixed rather than FSRS-predicted. The `abs(R − 0.33)` ordering labelled "DASH" in the spec has no DASH paper prescribing it: DASH is a memory model, not a priority rule. It should be described as overdue triage under the 12-minute daily budget. Given Latimier's result, effort spent tuning FSRS will likely matter less than effort spent on item validity.

## Sapling's weakest link is its measuring stick

Each piece of Sapling's evidence pipeline has a documented failure mode in the literature, and the pieces compound. The most serious is the post-test. As specified, it serves one reserved free-response item per concept from the same `check_items` pool the generator produced. That item is graded by the same LLM grader, and the post-test can open after **two days**. Kraft finds that researcher-designed outcomes aligned with the treatment inflate effects **2–4×** compared with broad tests, and that immediate measures run larger than delayed ones ([Kraft 2020](https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189X20912798)). Rismanchian's proctored versus unproctored reversal shows that how a test is administered can flip the sign of an effect. The current post-test is a good mechanism metric, but it is not an outcome that a skeptical reviewer would accept.

Item quality is the second risk. In medical education, LLM-written MCQs match human items on difficulty; the studies split on discrimination, and a 2026 meta-analysis favours human items on discrimination (SMD −0.70, very heterogeneous) ([PMC12881591](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12881591/); [Medical Teacher](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/0142159X.2026.2691072)). The consistent weaknesses are cueing flaws: in one study, "longest option is correct" appeared in **82–87%** of GPT-generated MCQs ([Michigan WIP](https://websites.umich.edu/~kevynct/pubs/L_S_2024_WorkInProgress_Question_Generation_CRFinal2.pdf)), and correct answers reuse words from the stem. These flaws raise scores without raising knowledge, which looks like learning. No study covers items generated from **unvetted student uploads with peer-sourced keys**, which is Sapling's case. The beta accepts that a wrong source produces a wrong key (A43), and the planned guard is students reporting bad items (#695). Under graded-checks-only, a wrong key corrupts the system's only mastery evidence. It penalizes students who are right and certifies students who are wrong, and a guard that depends on students noticing will miss most such errors. A 19-criterion item-writing-flaw rubric can be applied automatically at 97% agreement ([arXiv 2307.08161](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.08161)), so a linter is cheap to add.

The third risk is the grader. A meta-analysis of 890 LLM short-answer scoring results found that decoder-only LLMs trailed encoder models by about **0.37 QWK**, were sensitive to wording, and showed racial bias in high-stakes settings ([arXiv 2603.04820](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04820)). Percent agreement can coexist with mean scores **10–20 points** off human scores ([GEM 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.gem-1.33.pdf)). Every LLM grader tested was vulnerable to hidden prompt injection in student answers ([MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7102/15/11/1419)). Sapling's reported 0.98 agreement on a 45-case developer fixture set is a regression test, not a validity study. A grader risk specific to Sapling has been inferred but not measured: tutor and grader share a model family, so treated students who learn the tutor's phrasing could be rewarded for the phrasing rather than the understanding. If so, the measured effect would be biased upward in exactly the condition being evaluated.

## A within-student trial can prove learning at startup scale

Simple power arithmetic rules out the obvious design. The arithmetic here is the research notes' own, using standard formulas at α = .05 and 80% power. A two-arm between-student RCT needs about **786 students to detect d = 0.2**, or about 400 with a strong pretest covariate. One course of 50–150 students can detect only d ≥ 0.4–0.5, which Kraft's distribution says is implausible on a broad outcome. A within-student design changes this. Each consenting student has some concepts randomly assigned to the Sapling loop and comparable concepts to business-as-usual study, counterbalanced across students, and everyone is tested on all concepts later. Each student then serves as their own control. With a within-student correlation of 0.5 to 0.7, **about 120–200 students across 2–4 partnered courses detect 0.2–0.3 SD**, analysed with a crossed student-by-item random-effects model. The design has precedent: Lindsey et al. varied review scheduling within students and found a 16.5% gain over massed study and 10% over generic spaced review on a post-semester cumulative exam, persisting at one month ([Psych Science](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797613504302); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/579380)). It does not capture whole-product effects such as displacement or motivation, and spillover between related concepts biases it toward zero, so randomize weakly related concepts or whole units.

The evaluation should run in three stages. **Stage 0, over about two months, establishes measurement validity.** An instructor or TA audits a stratified sample of generated items for key errors and flaws; a flaw linter runs on every item, with option position randomized and option lengths balanced; 200–300 real, de-identified responses are double-scored by experts to report human–human agreement, LLM–human QWK, MAE and signed bias by item type and subgroup; and an injection red-team set becomes a standing test. **Stage 1, over one term, is the preregistered within-student trial.** Its primary outcome is a delayed test at one week or more on items written or vetted by instructors, kept out of the generator's pool, never shown during tutoring, and either selected-response or human-scored. Secondary outcomes are a four-week or end-of-term retention test, near- and far-transfer items, and course exam items mapped to concepts under a FERPA release. In-system accuracy and the current post-test are reported as mechanism measures. The same infrastructure can host cheap feature A/B tests. The most valuable one would fill the literature's gap: strict withholding versus short explanation followed by an attempt, judged on the delayed outcome. **Stage 2, the following year, is a multi-course crossover or between-student RCT** with an academic partner and an independent analyst. It should prespecify a business-as-usual comparison and, ideally, a generic-chatbot arm, aim for 400 or more students, and seek SBIR or foundation funding. For comparison, the RAND trial of Carnegie Learning's Cognitive Tutor cost about $6M and found about 0.2 SD only in its second implementation year ([EdSurge](https://www.edsurge.com/n/2013-08-27-the-nature-of-big-edtech-research); [arXiv 1802.08616](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1802.08616)). Eedi's model, a quick exploratory preprint followed by a larger four-arm trial, is the one to copy.

Credibility comes from process as much as design. Preregister hypotheses, the primary outcome and the analysis code. Report every outcome, including nulls; Kraft shows preregistered trials report smaller and more believable effects. Recruit through a faculty partner's IRB, which typically treats normal-educational-practice studies as exempt or expedited, though that is the IRB's call. Take research consent separately from the terms of service, and design so that no student is disadvantaged on graded work. The WWC "without reservations" rating requires random assignment and low attrition ([WWC handbook v5.0](https://memphis.edu/crep/pdfs/resources/wwc-handbook-ver5_0-0-508_1.pdf)).

## What remains unknown, and which citations to verify

Several questions central to Sapling have no answer yet. No college RCT shows a **positive unassisted effect at a delay of a month or more** from a student-facing LLM tutor. The best delayed result is Oreopoulos et al.'s marginal one-week gain in middle school. No study tests FSRS, or any scheduler, on concepts that are reviewed with a fresh item each time. No study measures the validity of LLM items generated from student-uploaded material, or of teach-back and MC-with-reason formats. No study measures whether a grader from the same model family as the tutor favours tutor-style phrasing. Per-step prediction, turn length, and Sapling's specific band values are all untested. Who benefits is contested: first-generation students were hurt more in Liu, high achievers more in Strömberg, and higher-baseline students gained more in Nigeria ([World Bank WP 11125](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/entities/publication/15e1ff08-15ae-4f7a-b2a8-d146e6c113ee/full)). No meta-analysis yet separates delayed unassisted outcomes from immediate assisted ones. A 2026 STEM meta-analysis using robust Bayesian correction shrank the GenAI effect to **μ ≈ 0.08** with large heterogeneity ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10462-026-11665-9)), which suggests the headline pooled effects of g ≈ 0.7–0.9 are largely publication bias. Finally, the "Agent Harness for Learning Things" design that motivated several contested features could not be located, so its own claims were not checked.

## Conclusion

Sapling's design was built to avoid the failure that 2025–2026 research has now documented at scale, and it largely succeeds on paper. The next risk is different. The system's tight self-consistency (one pipeline writing the items, keys, grades and post-tests) means it could report steady mastery gains that an independent test would not reproduce. Bastani's practice-versus-exam reversal shows how this happens. The highest-value work over the next two terms is therefore not new tutoring features but **separating the instrument from the treatment**. That means outcome items the generator never saw, scored by something other than the tutor's model family, at a delay long enough for forgetting to show up.

The research also shifts what a win looks like. A guarded tutor that only avoids harm matches the best current evidence, so an honest, preregistered 0.15 SD effect on a one-week-delayed, instructor-vetted test would place Sapling among the few college LLM tutors with credible durable-learning evidence. The within-student design makes that achievable with a few partnered courses rather than thousands of students. Its feature-level A/B tests can then address the literature's main open question, withholding versus brief explanation, using Sapling's own delayed outcomes.
